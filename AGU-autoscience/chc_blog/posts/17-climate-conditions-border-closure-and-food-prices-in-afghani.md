# Climate Conditions, Border Closure and Food Prices in Afghanistan

![](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/DSC00577-min.jpg)

***Image by Fahim Zaheer/ Climate Hazards Center*

The recent closure of Afghanistan’s border with Pakistan, combined with La Niña-driven below-average precipitation and localized dry spots, has created uncertainties for vulnerable households who are already affected by lack of employment opportunities, economic challenges, inflation and natural disasters, especially in areas near the border with Pakistan. The closure of Torkham port, a crucial transit artery for travelers and goods between Pakistan and Afghanistan, has been a significant blow to trade. The closure timing, just before Ramadan when food imports from Pakistan usually peak, has further exacerbated the situation.

In 2024, trade through Torkham port was valued at over $1.6 billion, highlighting its importance in the region’s economy. According to the Pakistan Chamber of Commerce, the closure has stranded about 5,000 trucks filled with fruits, food, and nonfood items, plus essential goods, causing at least $15 million in losses. If the border closure persists and the forecast below-average precipitation and above-average temperatures negatively impact crops, the pressures may further increase food prices and worsen food insecurity. These impacts may be particularly consequential for the people who depend on the agriculture sector for their income, and on bilateral trade and transit of goods through the Afghanistan-Pakistan border.

With the ongoing economic strains and potential disruptions in food supply chains, the upcoming months will be critical in food security outcomes. In addition to trade limitations, weather conditions will play a key role in shaping agricultural production and overall food availability. Current conditions remain favorable with near to above-average rainfall conditions in late February and early March improving soil moisture. Despite recent improvements, season-to-date rainfall accumulation remains below average (Figure 1). The 2024/25 season began in October with uncertainties regarding precipitation amounts and distribution. Rainfall was below-average in most areas, however the timing and distribution were sufficient for winter wheat planting and development. Looking at the progress of the season so far and the forecast rainfall conditions, winter wheat harvest  may be successful at a near-average to average level.

For spring crops, above-average rainfall in late February and early March has provided favorable conditions for planting. However, the extended outlook for the next several months indicates below-average precipitation based on most climate prediction centers, including[NOAA/CPC](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/international/nmme/probabilistic_seasonal/nmme_precip_probabilistic.shtml#CENTRAL%20ASIA). Forecast below-average precipitation and above-average temperature raises concerns about water availability and the potential impacts on rainfed crops  ([GEOGLAM, Crop Monitor for Early Warning](https://www.cropmonitor.org/crop-monitor-for-early-warning)). If below-average precipitation persists in spring and summer, wheat yields may significantly reduce, impacting food availability and nutrition.

Overall, climate hazards are a major concern. As per the International Organization for Migration (IOM) , about 3 million people have been displaced in the country due to climate disasters since 2021, with 11 million still at high risk. In February 2025, flash floods caused by heavy rain and hail in southern and south-western Afghanistan (areas of Fahar, Kandahar, and Helmand) disrupted livelihoods and led to loss of lives ([ECHO FLASH](https://erccportal.jrc.ec.europa.eu/ECHO-Products/Echo-Flash#/daily-flash-archive/5285)).

Food prices are another major factor shaping food security in the country. Afghanistan imports staple food items from its neighboring countries, mainly Kazakhstan and Iran. Volatility in international and national food prices is impacting consumers and deteriorating the purchasing power of people who rely on getting food from the market.

Despite the three-week closure of the Pakistan-Afghan border, stability of wheat grain and flour prices in most of Afghanistan’s markets is a testament to the market’s resilience. The injection of Kazakhstan’s wheat flour into Afghanistan’s markets has played a crucial role in maintaining stability.

So far, closure of the Pakistan and Afghanistan borders has not significantly affected the physical and economic access to staple food items in most parts of the country, including the provinces bordering Pakistan. The Afghanistan wheat production and flow map underscores the critical role of the Torkham border in the country’s food supply chain (Figure 2).

Afghanistan depends on imports from Pakistan for several staple foods, especially fresh fruits , vegetables (perishable), and rice. According to recent observations, trade flows for food items typically imported from Pakistan to Afghanistan have been disrupted, resulting in price increases . A notable price increase was reported in imported vegetables and fruits. Similarly, the price of locally produced vegetables also increased due to the reduced supply of imported vegetables. Regarding staple food, there are reports of a localized increase in imported rice prices (between 10 and 15 percent) compared to before the border closure period.

Since Afghanistan is more dependent on Kazakhstan than Pakistan for wheat grain and flour imports, prices for both high- and low-quality wheat have remained stable, with reduced imports from Pakistan offset by supplies from Kazakhstan. However, prices for fruits, vegetables, sugar, and dairy products have increased. These mostly come from outside Afghanistan, i.e., Malaysia, through Pakistan.

The border closure may be negatively impacting  food security of the undocumented returnees and poor people in Afghanistan, especially the people who live on the border with Pakistan. This recent problem at the border has left significant economic implications for the business community from both countries.

Among those stuck are dozens of patients on the Afghan side of the border who are waiting for permission to enter Pakistan for treatment, and  many people who are running small businesses and/or working as laborers in Pakistan. The situation is turning into huge financial losses for those trying to enter Pakistan.

As per WFP Afghanistan, variations in the exchange rate, global food prices, seasonal variations, and demand-supply dynamics are the main drivers of these price volatilities. In February 2025, the cost of wheat grain negligibly increased by 0.7 percent, rice (imported) by 2.2 percent, pulses by 2.7 percent, sugar by 1.9 percent, and salt by 3 percent. However, there is reason for optimism as the price of imported (high-quality) wheat flour, a key staple, and key vegetables, including tomatoes, potatoes, and onions, have all seen a decrease, with wheat flour dropping by 1.2 percent, tomatoes by 16 percent, potatoes by 4.1 percent, and onions by 4.7 percent.

As of February 2025, the national average price of imported wheat flour is AFN 30.2, a minor decrease of 1.2 percent compared to January 2025. Meanwhile, the current price is 6 percent lower than the same time last year and 25 percent below the 4-year average prices. Notably, cooking oil is the only commodity that has experienced stable prices, providing a sense of reassurance in an otherwise volatile market.

Year-on-year, most food prices have dropped, except for cooking oil, salt, and potatoes, which remain higher. However, food prices (excluding wheat grain and wheat flour) are significantly higher compared to the pre-COVID period. Similarly, except for wheat grain, both low- and high-quality wheat flour, and cooking oil, the prices of all other food commodities are notably higher than in June 2021. As of February 2025, the national average price of DAP increased by 0.8 percent compared to January 2025. This is 7 percent lower than 2024 prices but remains 37 percent higher than June 2021. Also, the average price of UREA is only one percent higher than the previous month, 10 percent lower than the same time last year, but 29 percent higher than June 2021.
