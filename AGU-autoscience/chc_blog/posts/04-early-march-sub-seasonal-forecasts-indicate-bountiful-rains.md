# Early March Sub-Seasonal Forecasts Indicate Bountiful Rains and a Good Start of Season in Kenya

**Introduction**: International Collaborators supporting improved climate services at the Kenya Meteorological Department

As described recently in Dr. Phillip Okello’s talk at the 72nd GHACOF in Nairobi (Figure 1), four groups are currently working closely with the Kenya Meteorological Department (KMD) to support improved monthly and sub-seasonal forecasts, agricultural advisories and long-lead extreme precipitation predictions. These groups are the Climate Hazards Center ([CHC](http://chc.ucsb.edu)) and University Center for Atmospheric Research Innovative Capacity Development Program ([ICDP](https://www.comet.ucar.edu/capacity-development)), the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology’s ([KIT](https://www.kit.edu/english/)) Institute of Meteorology and Climate Research – Department of Troposphere Research ([IMKTRO](https://www.imk-tro.kit.edu/english/index.php)), and [Rhiza Research](https://rhizaresearch.org/). Support from the Famine Early Warnings Systems Network, a KIT Excellence Award, a Fellowship of the SCHROFF Foundation, and the Gates Foundation is helping KMD expand their network of 3D-Printed Automatic Weather Stations (3D-PAWS,[blog1](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1840),[blog2](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1905)), improve sub-seasonal forecasts, and rapidly combine rainfall observations and predictions. In this post, we discuss results related to the start of the 2026 March-to-May long rains in Kenya.

**An excellent and early onset of rains across much of Kenya**

As discussed in a late February report by KMD ([here](https://meteo.go.ke/documents/2706/MAM_2026_Season_forecast_Onset_Update.pdf)), and by KMD Director Mr. Edward Muriuki in early March in a Monday Report video ([here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NOpSabHxN9A)), anomalous circulation patterns brought substantial rains to much of Kenya in late February. Based on KMD’s rainy season onset definition, 20 mm of rainfall within three consecutive days, with no dry spell exceeding seven (7) days thereafter, four regions were identified as experiencing an onset of the rainy season in February: A. counties in the Highlands West of the Rift Valley, Lake Victoria Basin, Central, and South Rift Valley, B. Highlands East of the Rift Valley including Nairobi County. C. The Southeastern Lowlands, and the South Coast. Given these observed onsets, our focus here is on rainfall prospects for March— though we also provide some results indicative of above-normal outcomes in April.

**The elusive wet long rains forecast conundrum and opportunities for identifying good long rains growing seasons**

While the predictable October-November-December (OND) short rains are well known to have distinctive sea surface temperature patterns associated with wet and dry seasons, the long rains provide a challenge for forecasters. Many dry March-April-May (MAM) seasons are known to be associated with a strong negative ‘Western V Gradient’ ([paper1](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2023EF003524)), which amplifies the east-west Indo-Pacific Walker Circulation and increases the frequency of poor rainy seasons ([paper2](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1029/2022EF003454)). However, wet MAM seasons, which offer agricultural opportunities, are not associated with clear SST signatures. The sub-seasonal Madden-Julian Oscillation (MJO) can contribute to both droughts ([paper3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00703-022-00948-9)) and extremely wet seasons ([paper4](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4433/9/12/472)). High frequency convectively coupled equatorial Kelvin waves can also drive precipitation extremes ([paper5](https://aseestant.ceon.rs/index.php/geopan/article/view/31132)).

Writing in early March 2026, we are interested in whether sub-seasonal forecasts can help us identify an early start of the MAM rainy season, and whether such forecasts might also help us gain insights into the middle part of the rainy season. To that end, we first present co-produced weekly ECMWF precipitation forecasts, for Kenya, in a system currently being run at KIT. These forecasts, expressed as anomalies from the median, show very wet conditions in early March, followed by persistent above-average rainfall through the beginning of April.

![Figure 2. March 2nd 2026 ECMWF S2S forecasts, from a github workflow system primarily developed by KIT and KMD. These forecasts in kgm-2 are equivalent to forecasts in mm per week.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/ECMWF_precip_anomalies_from_theMedian_from_March2nd2026_Mar26-300x177.png)

To relate these forecasts with gridded rainfall observations – version 3 of the Climate Hazards center InfraRed Precipitation with Stations ([CHIRPS3](https://chc.ucsb.edu/data/chirps3)) archive, 0.5° degree 2005–2026 ECMWF forecasts were downscaled to 0.05° resolution using the CHIRPS archive and quantile matching. This downscaling helps represent the influence of elevation and other local drivers of spatial rainfall gradients. While 0.05° forecast results will be examined later, we first present the ECMWF March forecasts, averaged over the entire country (x-axis), along with the observed CHIRPS values (Figure 3). These results are very exciting. First, the overall skill is high, with an R2of 0.69. It should also be noted that both very dry March forecasts (shown in pink, forecast rainfall < 50 mm) and very wet March months (shown in brown, forecast rainfall > 100 mm) discriminate outcomes very well. They hit 100% of the time, and have no false alarms. One wet March (2024, shown in green) was not predicted well. The red dot in Figure 3 shows that the current ECMWF forecast indicates a very wet March 2026, on average, for Kenya. This result is not so easy to see in the weekly forecasts shown in Figure 2. Also shown on Figure 3 is the 80th percentile confidence bound for March, based on the empirical 2005–2025 standard error. This simple metric implies that there is a 90% chance that average Kenyan March 2026 rainfall will be greater than ~70 mm – i.e. the March 2026 rains are very likely to be above normal.

![Figure 3. 2005–2026 downscaled ECMWF S2S precipitation forecasts for March, averaged over Kenya (x-axis) along with observed CHIRPS3 values (y-axis).](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Scatterplot_March_ECMWF_rainfall_forecasts_and_observations_for_Kenya_Mar26-300x245.png)

While promising some recovery from the very poor rains that came during OND of 2025 ([blog](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1905)), a short-lived respite in March might be followed by poor rains in April. When examining Figure 3, however, we noted that many of the well-predicted wet March months were also followed by very wet April outcomes (2013, 2018, 2020 and 2023 – but not 2010). Long rain seasons like 2013, 2018 2020 and 2023 were associated with elevated flooding risk, but also elevated agricultural opportunities. The relatively short period of record explored in Figure 3 (2005–2025) makes it a little hard to establish such relationships. But 45 years of observed CHIRPS3 data (1981–2025) also supports this relationship.**These results suggest exciting opportunities for the early identification of poor or good growing seasons, as well as seasons with elevated flood risk. The pink dots in Figure 4 suggest that if we think March rains will be low, then we should also be worried that April rains may be below normal. Conversely, the brown dots in Figure 4 suggest that if we think March rains will be heavy, then we may also be likely to see good crop growing and elevated flood risk – as in 2018, 2020 and 2023.**

Previous studies have linked early and late onsets to wetter or drier long rains seasons (e.g.[paper6](https://rmets.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002%2Fjoc.8528),[paper7](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wace.2018.05.003),[paper8](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/hydr/27/3/JHM-D-25-0080.1.xml)). Figure 3 suggests that these early onsets may be predictable, which may give us a useful early indication of the overall performance for the season. More specifically, Figure 4 suggests that many extreme Aprils may be presaged by extreme March conditions. March outcomes can be predicted with sub-seasonal models, monitored usingobservations, or evaluated using combinations of forecasts and observations. While past research into dry MAM seasons have described the contribution of delayed onsets ([paper9](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41612-019-0091-7)), which may be related to the Western V Gradient ([paper1](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2023EF003524)) and Walker Circulation intensification ([paper2](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1029/2022EF003454)), there has been little research focused on the sub-seasonal predictability of extreme April rainy seasons in Kenya. Kilavi et al. ([paper4](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4433/9/12/472)) described the contribution of the MJO to the extreme 2018 rains. What is exciting about Figure 3 is that the wet March rains in 2018 (and 2020 and 2023) were anticipated well by the ECMWF model.

The data shown in Figure 4 suggests further potential predictability, based on persistence between March and April. The brown dots are selected based on March values exceeding the 80th percentile bound shown in Figure 3 (87 mm). This plot suggests that when March rainfall outcomes have been greater than 87 mm, most April averages have been above normal (9 out of 13 times). A similar tilt in the odds is seen for very dry March seasons (pink circles). Dry March outcomes are often followed by dry April precipitation. A few years (1997 and 2024) saw extreme April rains and below-average March totals (green circles in Figure 4).

While more research into these results is warranted, they do suggest that wet March rains are likely to be associated with longer and wetter growing seasons, due to the persistence of above normal rainfall from one month to the next. Combine this relationship with the good forecast skill for March (Figure 3), and we have a viable path forward to identifying MAM seasons with high chances of good agricultural outcomes.

**Assessing the likelihood of an early March Start of Season for Kenya, based on ECMWF S2S forecasts**

We next present downscaled ECMWF forecasts for the first dekad of March and the sum of the second and third dekads of March (March 1–10 and March 11–31). For the first dekad, we see bountiful rains across much of the country (Figure 5, left). Except for the northwest and far eastern part of the country, almost the whole country is expected to receive copious rainfall. Current reports and satellite estimates confirm extreme rains in early March. In fact, rains have been so heavy that wet soils and extreme weather may be inhibiting land preparation and planting. ECMWF forecasts for the remainder of the month (Figure 5, right) suggest continued rainfall, with much of the country expected to get more than 50 mm of rainfall.

These forecasts are based on the ensemble average ECMWF forecast, which is then translated to quantiles, and matched to historical CHIRPS3 values. Hence Figure 5 shows the most likely rainfall outcomes. One would expect that there is about a 50% chance that actual rainfall amounts might fall above or below these values. To explore a lower confidence bound, we can subtract a map of per-pixel standard errors from the maps shown in Figure 5. We calculate the 2005–2025 per-pixel (0.05°) standard error for the March 1–10 and March 11–31 forecasts, and then subtract these values from the mean forecast in Figure 5. While rainfall is certainly not normally distributed, the anomalies from the forecast will be closer to a normal distribution. If the errors are normally distributed, then there will be a 16% chance of the rain being less than the forecast minus 1 standard error, and an 84% chance the actual rainfall total will be above the forecast minus 1 standard error value. These probabilities are approximated, but this approach provides a defensible, easy-to-calculate and easy-to-understand lower bound. Figure 6 shows simple maps in which a pixel has been colored cyan if the March 1–10 rains are expected to exceed 25 mm and the March 11–31 rains are expected to exceed 20 mm. The ‘expected’ criteria is based on the forecast minus 1 standard error value being at least 25 or 20 mm.

The two criteria— a one dekad wet spell followed by a 2 dekad period of at least moderate rainfall, are widely used within the Water Requirement Satisfaction Index (WRSI) model. Together, these signify a Start of Season, which typically provides enough moisture for germinating seeds to reach the early vegetative stage.

WRSI modeling at the CHC, driven with CHIRPS3 and the CHC SubC ensemble (SubC[here](https://chc.ucsb.edu/monitoring/subx), WRSI[here](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/will/Season_WRSI_Monitoring/East_Africa/MarNov_LongRains/Sorghum/with_Forecast/SubX/)), also shows an observed or expected start to the rainy season, much earlier than normal (Figure 7 left and middle). These results are based on CHIRPS observations, 3 dekads of SubC forecasts, and all prior years of CHIRPS data for the remainder of the season, as described in[paper6](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/hydr/23/8/JHM-D-21-0184.1.xml)and[paper7](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/2752-5295/adcadf/meta). The SoS map (Figure 7 left) suggests a Start of Season in February or early March across most of Kenya, which is 2-to-3 dekads earlier than normal (Figure 7, middle). Across all model forecast systems— including ECMWF and SubC models— the accuracy of rainfall forecasts declines for forecasts made several weeks in advance, and thus the rainfall distribution during late March, for example, is of higher uncertainty. While it is possible that early starts could be followed by crop water deficits, the CHC WRSI modeling system — driven with the SubC forecasts and historical data, suggests low probability of poor WRSI outcomes (Figure 7, right). These results suggest that there is a less than 20% chance of below-normal (bottom tercile) WRSI outcomes at the end of the season, i.e. a reduced probability of good crop water satisfaction outcomes.
