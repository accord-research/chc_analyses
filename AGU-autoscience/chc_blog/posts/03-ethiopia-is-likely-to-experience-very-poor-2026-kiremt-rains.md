# Ethiopia is Likely to Experience Very Poor 2026 Kiremt Rains

**Authors:** Chris Funk, Diriba Korecha, Asaminew Teshome, Johnson Barasa, Chris Shitote, Daniella Alaso, Will Turner and Greg Husak

Key Messages

- Over the last few months the strength of El Niño grew rapidly, with Relative Oceanic Niño Index values reaching +1.25°C by early June.
- Observed CHIRPS rainfall for April-to-June, enhanced with ~90 Ethiopian Meteorological Institute stations, indicate below-normal rains.
- Dynamic weather model forecasts for the next 15-to-30 days indicate a high probability of very poor July rainfall. El Niño analog-based forecasts anticipate poor June-to-September rains across most of the country.
- Combining these sources of information suggests that much of Ethiopia is very likely to experience very poor June-to-September rainfall.

**Summary**: This short blog post updates a recent (June 3rd) CHC[blog](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1982)that explored ‘What the 2026 El Niño may mean for Ethiopia’s June–September Kiremt Rainy Season’. In this post, El Niño analog-based analyses, using a conservative set of nine moderate-to-strong El Niños, suggested poor outcomes for the June-to-September Kiremt rains in Ethiopia. We now add two new sources of information: 1) enhanced rainfall observations through the end of June and 2) subseasonal precipitation forecasts for July. El Niño sea surface temperatures are warming very rapidly, and causing El Niño-related deficits in rainfall observations and weather forecasts. Observed May and June rainfall totals were very low over much of Ethiopia, coastal Kenya, Western Kenya, Uganda and South Sudan. Downscaled dynamic weather model predictions for July, indicate very low rains in the central-eastern ‘Kiremt’ rainfall region. By combining the rainfall observations for June, weather model forecasts for July, and analog-based projections for August-September we produce outlooks for the June-to-September Kiremt rains over Ethiopia. These projections indicate very widespread dry conditions over most of the Kiremt rainy season regions, with very low seasonally accumulated rainfall totals. We therefore conclude that Ethiopia is very likely to experience very poor Kiremt rains in 2026.

**Analysis Overview**

Over the past several years, the Famine Early Warning Systems Network (FEWS NET) science team has developed two powerful tools that can rapidly combine rainfall observations and forecasts.The ‘Early Estimates’ rainfall products ([Recent Rainfall](https://chc.ucsb.edu/monitoring/early-estimates),[Seasonal Rainfall](https://chc.ucsb.edu/monitoring/early-estimates/seasonal)) combine[CHIRPS3](https://chc.ucsb.edu/data/chirps3)observations with 15-day downscaled[CHIRPS-GEFS](https://chc.ucsb.edu/data/chirps-gefs)rainfall forecasts. They have near-global coverage, and are routinely updated. The ‘Seasonal Monitoring and Probability Generator’ ‘SMPG’ products for Kenya and Ethiopia are updated every dekad, and customized for specific countries and seasons. In addition to CHIRPS3 and CHIRPS-GEFS, they incorporate 1) extra station observations, 2) sub-seasonal forecasts looking out 30 days, and 3) analog years that encapsulate long term climate assumptions. At present, analog years represent moderate-to-very strong El Niño conditions, as described in the June 3rd, 2026 CHC[blog](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1982). SMPG results for Kenya and Ethiopia can be found[here](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/CountryMonitors/East_Africa/Regional/2026/SMPG_Plots/Kenya/)and[here](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/CountryMonitors/East_Africa/Regional/2026/SMPG_Plots/Ethiopia/).

A detailed presentation on the CHC Early Estimates can be found at these links ([mp4](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/CountryMonitors/East_Africa/Ethiopia/Agroclimate_Special_Report/2026/June/Ethiopia_Likely_To_Experience_Very_Poor_Kiremt_Rains_In_2026.mp4),[ppt](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/CountryMonitors/East_Africa/Ethiopia/Agroclimate_Special_Report/2026/June/Ethiopia_Likely_To_Experience_Very_Poor_Kiremt_Rains_In_2026.pptx)). In addition to describing the Kiremt 2026 outlook, the presentation also describes how the Early Estimates and SMPG tools can combine observations and forecasts.

Between May and June 2026, equatorial East Pacific sea surface temperature anomalies increased dramatically, with anomalies reaching +1.8°C in the last week of June (Figure 1). Similar results can be viewed at the Australia Bureau of Meteorology (BoM,[link](https://www.bom.gov.au/climate/influences/graphs/index.rnino.shtml?index=nino34&period=weekly)). These values are raw ‘traditional’ (non-adjusted) Niño3.4 sea surface temperature anomalies, not the adjusted Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI) values. While 2026 RONI values are cooler (BoM[link](https://www.bom.gov.au/climate/influences/graphs/index.rnino.shtml?index=rnino34&period=weekly), slide 11[ppt](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/CountryMonitors/East_Africa/Ethiopia/Agroclimate_Special_Report/2026/June/Ethiopia_Likely_To_Experience_Very_Poor_Kiremt_Rains_In_2026.pptx)), they are still very warm (>+1.2°C), and also show a rapid increase in east Pacific sea surface temperatures. Current Niño3.4 temperatures are substantially warmer than in 2015 and 1987, and we appear to be on track for a strong El Niño event.

Anomalies were calculated using a 1991-2020 baseline.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Blog_Figure1-300x244.png)

The rapid warming of the East Pacific helps explain the emergence of poor rains within western Kenya, Uganda, South Sudan and Ethiopia (slides 4-6[ppt](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/CountryMonitors/East_Africa/Ethiopia/Agroclimate_Special_Report/2026/June/Ethiopia_Likely_To_Experience_Very_Poor_Kiremt_Rains_In_2026.pptx)), and recent weather forecasts suggest continued dry conditions in July (slides 7-8[ppt](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/CountryMonitors/East_Africa/Ethiopia/Agroclimate_Special_Report/2026/June/Ethiopia_Likely_To_Experience_Very_Poor_Kiremt_Rains_In_2026.pptx)). These indicators combine with historical El Niño impacts (slides 9[ppt](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/CountryMonitors/East_Africa/Ethiopia/Agroclimate_Special_Report/2026/June/Ethiopia_Likely_To_Experience_Very_Poor_Kiremt_Rains_In_2026.pptx)) to provide convergent evidence pointing to dry outcomes in Ethiopia.

To track conditions over Ethiopia, we define an El Niño-impacted region over central and eastern Ethiopia (upper-right inset in Figure 2). We can then compare observed rainfall with the long term mean, and see that substantial rainfall deficits begin to accrue in mid-May, and persist through the end of June (blue bars). Weather forecasts for July suggest that below-normal rains will continue through July. Historically, these July forecasts have worked fairly well (R2= 0.48) and the multi-model ensemble forecast for July 2026 is similar to 2002 and 2015, when observed rainfall totals were very low (slide 8[ppt](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/CountryMonitors/East_Africa/Ethiopia/Agroclimate_Special_Report/2026/June/Ethiopia_Likely_To_Experience_Very_Poor_Kiremt_Rains_In_2026.pptx)).

Using the SMPG tool, we can combine the observations, downscaled dynamic model forecasts and El Niño analog assumptions for, respectively, June, July, and August-September (Figure 3). While this seems relatively simple, it stacks a great deal of information obtained via great human effort and innovation: the EMI observation network, the CHIRPS rainfall estimation process, multiple dynamic model forecast systems, and years of El Niño-Ethiopia teleconnection research. Figure 3 shows June-to-September Kiremt-region SMPG rainfall accumulations. In June, the observations (heavy blue line) identified 62 mm of rain, substantially less than the 111 mm long term average. The below-normal weather forecast totals for July (heavy pink line) increase modestly, to 175 mm, but this increases the gap when compared to the long-term average (330 mm). Beginning with the first dekad of July, nine scenarios, corresponding to nine moderate-to-strong El Niños, are then used to run out nine likely scenarios for August and September. These are shown with thin colored lines. What these results tell us is that if the monthly weather forecasts for July verify, then recovery in August and September is very unlikely. August-September rains from one fairly wet analog year (1994) brings the total to just below 600 mm, but the other analogs produce outcomes near 500 mm. Very dry Kiremt seasons like 1987 and 2015 had seasonal totals of around 550.

![Figure 3. June-to-September SMPG seasonal accumulations for the Kiremt region shown in Figure 2.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Blog_Figure3-300x125.png)

The SMPG tool can be used to repeat the process shown in Figure 3 for a dense set of administrative areas. For each area, the observed June rainfall, downscaled July weather forecasts, and nine El Niño analog rainfall for August and September can be combined to produce a single summary value. This information can be visualized as a map. Figure 4 expresses these values as percentiles, with 0 being the driest value since 1981, and 100 being the wettest. Almost the entire Kiremt region is expected to be very dry, with percentile values of less than 21%. Many key crop growing areas are expected to be exceptionally dry, with 0-to-11 percentile values. The 20, 10, and 5 percentile values correspond to 1-in-5, 1-in-10 and 1-in-20 year dry seasons. The expectation for wide-spread extremely low rainfall comes from stacking low June rainfall observations  (slide 4[ppt](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/CountryMonitors/East_Africa/Ethiopia/Agroclimate_Special_Report/2026/June/Ethiopia_Likely_To_Experience_Very_Poor_Kiremt_Rains_In_2026.pptx)), dry dynamic model predictions (slide 7[ppt](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/CountryMonitors/East_Africa/Ethiopia/Agroclimate_Special_Report/2026/June/Ethiopia_Likely_To_Experience_Very_Poor_Kiremt_Rains_In_2026.pptx)), and low El Niño analog rains in August and September. Many of these areas are important cropping regions that have dense populations (inset slide 4[ppt](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/CountryMonitors/East_Africa/Ethiopia/Agroclimate_Special_Report/2026/June/Ethiopia_Likely_To_Experience_Very_Poor_Kiremt_Rains_In_2026.pptx)).

![Figure 4. End-of-season projected Kiremt (June-to-September) rainfall totals, expressed as percentiles using a 1981-to-2025 baseline. These projections combine June rainfall observations, July weather forecasts, and August-September analog rainfall totals from nine El Niño years.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Blog_Figure_4-300x268.jpeg)

Results similar to Figure 3 and 4 were produced using just the June observations and July-to-September El Niño analog rainfall. While not as severe as the outlooks with dynamic model inputs for July, the overall story remains similar. Almost the entire Kiremt region will likely receive below-normal Kiremt rains (slide 15[ppt](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/CountryMonitors/East_Africa/Ethiopia/Agroclimate_Special_Report/2026/June/Ethiopia_Likely_To_Experience_Very_Poor_Kiremt_Rains_In_2026.pptx)), and the regionally averaged Kiremt rainy season outcome (592 mm) is wetter than 2015 and 1987, but still very dry.

June 26th to July 20th Early Estimates over East Africa indicate exceptional aridity, with Ethiopian rainfall anomalies of less than -100 mm over much of the Kiremt region (Figure 5). The same rainfall amounts, expressed as standardized precipitation index values (Figure 6) are exceptionally low (less than -2 SPI) across most of the country. After the end of July, the long-term mean rainfall across our focus region declines (heavy red line, Figure 2), and a strengthening El Niño will suppress rains in August and September. Given low observed rain in June, predicted low rainfall in July (Figure 5 and 6), and expected El Niño-driven deficits in August and September, it appears very likely that the 2026 Kiremt rains will be very poor.
