# A Warming World Brings Costly Desiccation and Inundation, but Attribution can Inform Prediction

Chris Funk, Emily Williams, Laura Harrison, Robert Saldivar, Pete Peterson, Shraddhanand Shukla,  Frank Davenport and Gregory Husak

As the Earth rapidly warms, climate is becoming more extreme, leading to billions of dollars in losses, and impacting millions of vulnerable people all over the world. According to one just-published paper (Williams et al.[2023](https://doi.org/10.1029/2022EF002943)), “**t***he Southwestern United States (U.S.) has been in a megadrought since 2000, characterized by below-average precipitation and elevated temperatures (A. P. Williams et al.,*[2020](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaz9600)*,*[2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41558-022-01290-z)*). During this time, there have been 13 “billion-dollar” drought-related events in the region (NOAA,*[2022](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/monitoring/monthly-report/drought/202008)*). Since 2002, the greater Four Corners region of the Southwestern U.S.—an area comprising Arizona, Utah, Colorado, and New Mexico, and encompassing much of the Colorado Plateau—has consistently experienced some form of drought (USDM,*[2022](https://droughtmonitor.unl.edu/DmData/TimeSeries.aspx)*). In 2018 and 2020, more than half of the Four Corners was in severe or exceptional drought, associated with drying vegetation, crop and pasture loss, increased fire activity, and reduced water availability (Dannenberg et al.,*[2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.16214)*; Mankin et al.,*[2021](https://www.drought.gov/sites/default/files/2021-09/NOAA-Drought-Task-Force-IV-Southwest-Drought-Report-9-23-21.pdf)*; USDM,*[2022](https://droughtmonitor.unl.edu/DmData/TimeSeries.aspx)*)*”. In East Africa, a climate change-enhanced three-year La Niña produced an unprecedented sequence of droughts that pushed millions of people into extreme hunger, killed more than nine million livestock, and required more than $1.8 billion dollars in US humanitarian relief. These droughts came on the heels of intense flooding in 2019 and 2020 and the worst desert locust outbreak in 75 years. Floods in the US also pose severe risks. For example, in 2022, in Texas, 12 extreme storms produced $20 billion dollars in losses (NOAA,[2023](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/billions/time-series/TX)). In 2017, Hurricane Harvey pummeled the coast of Texas and Louisiana in late August.Houston received more than 50 inches of rain from Harvey (Funk,[2021](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/drought-flood-fire/96E0EB1519F5175B68079D294D0B0E93)). More than 32,000 people were forced to abandon their homes and sleep in shelters (NPR,[2017](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2017/08/30/547227788/harvey-makes-landfall-again-in-louisiana)).

Yet, while most people realize that droughts, flood and fires are becoming more common, not enough people are thinking about how understanding the scientific links between warming and extremes can lead to better and more actionable predictions.

**Warmer air leads to predictable desiccation impacts**

Over land, increasing air temperatures increase the atmosphere’s ability to hold moisture. During storms and hurricanes, this can increase the intensity of extreme precipitation (Emori and Brown,[2005](https://doi.org/10.1029/2005GL023272), Knutson et al. 2019,Risser and Wehner[2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/2017GL075888)). During droughts,warmer air temperatures are tightly coupled with increasing Vapor Pressure Deficits, or VPD. High VPD values desiccate plants, so they often close stomata and reduce photosynthesis and biomass production(Dannenberg et al.,[2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.16214); Grossiord et al.,[2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.16485); Novick et al.,[2016a](https://doi.org/10.1038/nclimate3114)).  High VPD values are closely linked tolower moisture levels and higher flammability of dead vegetation, which, in turn, can be related to the extent of wildfire (Nolan,[2016b](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2016GL068614)). Firee month VPD has been found to be the dominant driver of fire frequencies and sizes across the Western US (Buch[2023](https://www.authorea.com/doi/full/10.1002/essoar.10512545.1)).

For farmers, ranchers and pastoralists, a critical question is ‘how damaging is a degree or two of warming’? Using satellite vegetation observations in the Four Corners region of the western US, a new study[(Williams et al. 2023)](https://doi.org/10.1029/2022EF002943)shows that the influence of high air temperatures and VPD values can be as important as precipitation. Climate-change driven increases in air temperatures and VPD have “**contributed to large and rapidly increasing reductions in rangeland productivity, with the greatest sensitivity in areas receiving moderate amounts of precipitation**”. Going further, the study links moisture stress to rangeland productivity, measured in pounds per acre, showing that “**relatively modest temperature increases have roughly doubled the magnitude of vegetation productivity deficits in recent years**.” For example, in 2020, moderate precipitation Four Corners grasslands produced an estimated 601 pounds of grass per acre, 170 pounds less than average. Human-induced VPD increases were estimated to have contributed to about half of this reduction (~95 pounds).

One take-away from this study is that a few degrees of warming can have big impacts on rangeland productivity. But another important message is that there are likely opportunities for predicting pasture health. As vegetation tends to reach peak greenness in August across most of this region, antecedent air temperatures, VPD, precipitation, and satellite observations of vegetation can be used to anticipate future rangeland health.

**Warmer ocean temperatures to opportunities for long-lead prediction**

Within the tropical Pacific, more frequent very warm sea surface temperatures (SST) have contributed to stronger El Niños and La Niñas (Funk et al.[2018](https://rmets.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/qj.3266); Funk[2021](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/drought-flood-fire/96E0EB1519F5175B68079D294D0B0E93)), but these predictable extremes create opportunities for proactive interventions (Funk et al.[2023a](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2023EF003524)). For the southwestern US and East Africa, the strong La Niñas associated with frequent poor rainy seasons can be predicted up to eight months in advance, if one takes into account forecasts of exceptionally warm west Pacific SST (Funk et al.[2023a](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2023EF003524)). During the 13 La Niña events since 1997, warming in the west Pacific causes La Niña-like conditions to persist into spring, causing persistent low precipitation in East Africa (Funk[2023b](https://www.authorea.com/users/580186/articles/621943-frequent-but-predictable-droughts-in-east-africa-driven-by-a-walker-circulation-intensification)) and the southern[US](https://www.psl.noaa.gov/cgi-bin/data/usclimdivs/climdiv.pl?variab=Precipitation&type=2&base=7&mon1=10&mon2=5&iy%5B1%5D=1998&iy%5B2%5D=1999&iy%5B3%5D=2000&iy%5B4%5D=2005&iy%5B5%5D=2007&iy%5B6%5D=2008&iy%5B7%5D=2010&iy%5B8%5D=2011&iy%5B9%5D=2016&iy%5B10%5D=2017&iy%5B11%5D=2020&iy%5B12%5D=2021&iy%5B13%5D=2022&iy%5B14%5D=&iy%5B15%5D=&iy%5B16%5D=&iy%5B17%5D=&iy%5B18%5D=&iy%5B19%5D=&iy%5B20%5D=&iy%5B21%5D=&iy%5B22%5D=&iy%5B23%5D=&iy%5B24%5D=&iy%5B25%5D=&iy%5B26%5D=&iy%5B27%5D=&iy%5B28%5D=&iy%5B29%5D=&iy%5B30%5D=&irange1=&irange2=&xlow=&xhi=&xint=&iunits=1&scale=100&iwhite=1&iswitch=0&Submit=Create+Plot). But since 1997, there have also been nine El Niño events. Only five of the last 26 years have not been a La Niña or El Niño (NOAA,[2023](https://origin.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/ensostuff/ONI_v5.php)). A plot of standardized equatorial Indo-Pacific SST (Figure 1) allows us to visualize the interaction of these events with climate change. There is a marked tendency for warming, but the locations of intense warming sway west and east, with the pulse of La Niñas and El Niños, offering opportunities for long-lead prediction.

**The interaction of El Niños, La Niñas and VPD increases risk**

The two risk conduits discussed here cross paths. For example, when in March-April-May of 2022 East Africa experienced its worst La Niña-driven drought since (at least) 1950 (FEWS NET[2022](https://fews.net/sites/default/files/Joint%20Statement%20Horn%20of%20Africa%209%20June%202022.pdf)), elevated VPD amplified the impact of rainfall deficits, as in the southern US in 2020[(Williams et al. 2023)](https://doi.org/10.1029/2022EF002943). In 2023,a combination of climate change and the influence of El Niño brought the warmest July on record ([NASA GISS](https://www.nasa.gov/press-release/nasa-clocks-july-2023-as-hottest-month-on-record-ever-since-1880)), and almost 30% of our planet experienced exceptional warm air or sea temperatures (Figure 2A). The July 2023 area of exceptional warmth (28%) was almost twice the previous maxima of 17% observed in 2016. Over the southwestern United States these exceptional temperatures appear associated with record-breaking VPD*(Figure 2B). The average VPD estimate across the southwest United States was ~3.4 kPa, substantially higher than previous very arid July values of ~3 kPa in 2002, 2004 and 2020. Going forward, predictable large-scale swings in climate (Figure 1) will produce more extreme atmospheric desiccation (Figure 2B).As ‘*climate science catches up with climate change*’ (Fernandez,[2023](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2023/07/230721113141.htm)), it is important that we develop now improved scientific frameworks for anticipating and mitigating these shocks.

*The data shown in Figure 2B are from a new experimental ‘CHIRTS-ERA5’ data set. This data set is based on the ERA5 reanalysis, but with the temperature fields adjusted to match the month Tmax means and standard deviations from the 1983-2016 Climate Hazards Infrared Temperatures with Stations (CHIRTS) [data set](https://chc.ucsb.edu/data/chirtsmonthly). The 1983-2016 correlation between the southwestern US areally averaged CHIRTS and CHIRTS-ERA5 July VPD time-series were 0.96.
