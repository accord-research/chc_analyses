# Exploring and Anticipating Extreme East African Short Rains in A Warming World with the Indo-Warm Pool Heating Gradient – with an OND 2024 outlook

Chris Funk, Laura Harrison, Gideon Galu, Diriba Korecha, Chris Shitote and Greg Husak

**Synopsis**

This is a technical post detailing a new approach and associated justification for forecasting precipitation across eastern East Africa. This technical post is intended to facilitate discussions with the East African climate forecast community. To support these detailed discussions, this post is longer and more technical than most previous CHC blog posts. For readers primarily interested in the outlook for October-November-December 2024, we suggest focusing on Section 1 (Figure 1), Section 4.2 (Figure 3C), Section 4.3 (Figure 4B), Section 4.5 (Figure 5C) and Section 5. We briefly summarize these results here.

**Section 1, Figure 1.**Eastern Horn rains have been extremely volatile since late 2016, swinging between extremely wet and dry seasons. The CHC’s latest forecast for October-November-December (OND 2024) suggests that yet-another dry season is likely.

**Section 4.2, Figure 3C.**We can gain insights into East Africa’s volatile short rainy seasons by looking at time series of two major climate drivers – the east-west gradient of Indian Ocean sea surface temperatures, measured using the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD), and the gradient of Pacific sea surface temperatures, measured using the West Pacific Gradient (WPG). The OND 2024 IOD and WPG forecasts are both negative, indicating that another dry season is likely.

**Section 4.3 Figure 4B.**A new measure ‘the Indo-Warm pool Heating Gradient’ (IWHG) contrasts the difference in raininess between the western Indian Ocean and Warm Pool area near Indonesia. Positive and negative IWHG values do an excellent job of tracking wet and dry short rainy season outcomes. The July forecast of OND 2024 IWHG values indicates a strong probability of below-normal eastern Horn rains.

**Section 4.5 Figure 5C.**Regression-based maps translate likely and worst case IWHG scenarios into percent of average CHIRPS anomalies. Central-Eastern Kenya, Somalia and parts of southern Ethiopia are likely to experience substantial rainfall deficits.

**Section 5 – Conclusion.**Forecasts for OND 2024 look similar to 2010, 2016, 2021 and 2022 – with the expectation of very negative WPG conditions and a moderate negative IOD. We should anticipate similar dryness, and, while not detailed here, recognize that there is also an elevated chance of below-normal March-April-May 2025 rains as well.

1. **Introduction**

**1.1 Recent droughts and extreme rainfall in eastern Horn of Africa**

During the 16 rainy seasons since October-November-December (OND) of 2016, the eastern Horn of Africa (eHorn) has experienced an exceptional sequence of extreme rainy seasons, with 8 dry seasons, 6 wet seasons, and just 2 normal rainy seasons (Figure 1). Figure 1 also shows a drought forecast for OND 2024, which will be described below.

![Figure 1. eHorn MAM and SPI, based on CHIRPS rainfall data, centered on a 1991-2020 baseline. The red dots in 2024 display a forecast for OND, with 80% confidence intervals – which will be described below. The dashed lines at SPI values of -0.44Z or +0.44Z correspond to the bottom and top tercile ranges, i.e. a one-in-three year dry or wet season.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/BarPlot_EasternEA_OND_MAM_CHIRPS_SPI_recent.png)

In 2016/17[(Funk et al 2018, 2019)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ShZoVQ)and 2020/22[(Funk et al 2023b, 2023a)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?tslBOZ)climate change-enhanced western Pacific sea surface temperatures (SST) amplified the influence of La Niña, leading to hazardous multi-season droughts. Drought conditions during the sequence of five dry OND and March-April-May (MAM) seasons from late 2020 through 2022-22 were exceptionally intense, persistent, extensive and hot, due to an exceptional multi-year La NiñaNina[(Anderson et al 2023)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?2egYRI). The situation devastated household incomes and livelihoods , as crops failed, water sources dried up, and cumulative shocks killed more than eight million livestock. Millions of people faced the threat of starvation, and emergency humanitarian relief efforts required more than $2 billion USD[(ICPAC et al 2022)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?iVuIfD)([link](https://fews.net/sites/default/files/Joint%20Statement%20Horn%20of%20Africa%209%20June%202022.pdf)). Flooding from extreme rains has also caused displacement, damages, and fatalities in recent years. Driving factors have been the Madden-Julian Oscillation, in MAM 2018[(Kilavi et al 2018)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?lNhsfD), positive Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) conditions, in OND 2019[(Nicholson et al 2022)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?rYftvc), and the IOD with a strong El Niño in OND 2023.

**1.2 A new, physically-based indicator for seasonal rainfall extremes**

These recent extremes provide potential opportunities for improving predictions, proactive risk management, and agricultural and water management outcomes. Here, focusing on OND rains, we explore the use of a new**I**ndo-**W**arm Pool**H**eating**G**radient (IWHG) indicator to understand and predict extreme eHorn rains.

The eHorn region, defined here as Kenya, Ethiopia and Somalia east and south of 38°E and 8°N, experiences two rainy seasons a year, the short OND and long MAM seasons. The OND season is more predictable. Yet the relationships between eHorn OND rains and Indo-Pacific sea surface temperatures have shifted over time, which can make rainfall forecasting challenging[(Nicholson 2015)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?xGJRoa). One recent study found that August forecasts of OND rains, based on the IOD and Niño3.4 SSTs, explained 40% the observed precipitation variance[(Kolstad and MacLeod 2022)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?WMz1i9), with changes in IOD conditions between August and OND being an important source of error. Another study[(Funk et al 2023b)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?CtQC1q), focused on using the West Pacific Gradient (WPG, the difference in standardized SST in equatorial West Pacific and the Niño3.4 region) to anticipate La Niña-related droughts at very long-lead times. Such droughts, as in OND 2010, 2016, 2020, 2021 and 2022, are particularly dangerous because they are often followed by dry MAM seasons the following year.

Collaboration between the Climate Hazards Center and the IGAD Climate Prediction and Applications Center (ICPAC) has explored this relationship using ERA5 reanalysis “atmospheric heating”[(Funk et al 2023a)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?9fp0dQ). To calculate atmospheric heating (Figure 2), we add up the different heat terms that heat the atmosphere – with the two largest terms being the latent heat released during precipitation and convergence of heat from the surrounding atmosphere. Our long rains paper showed that during most of the MAM seasons that followed the 12 OND La Niñas between 1998 and 2021, atmospheric heating over the Indo-Pacific ‘Warm Pool’ – the area around Indonesia – was anomalously strong. This amplification of the Walker Circulation reduced rainfall over the eHorn in MAM. While a dry MAM does not follow all OND La Niñas (e.g. MAM 2023), the frequent occurrence of this outcome – and high stakes of sequential drought impacts in this region – means that OND La Niñas are a useful warning indicator of probable back-to-back dry seasons.

**1.3 The current threat of drought, in OND 2024 and beyond**

We currently face the anticipated emergence of another La Niña that could potentially impact both the short and long rains seasons in the eastern Horn. A La Niña event will very likely develop by OND 2024 (an 84% chance, according to the June[CPC/IRI official forecast](https://iri.columbia.edu/our-expertise/climate/forecasts/enso/current/?enso_tab=enso-cpc_plume)). If it develops, strong equatorial Pacific SST gradients will likely be present due to the warming trend in the western Pacific.

Negative IOD and La Niña conditions often occur in tandem. When these are strong they can reinforce anomalous atmospheric patterns responsible for dry OND seasons in the eastern Horn. NMME model forecasts for the Indian Ocean, as of early July 2024, predict that moderately negative IOD conditions will occur during OND 2024, with a dipole gradient of -0.4C. Recent declines in the observed IOD index support an outlook for a weak to moderate strength negative IOD during OND, and highlight the need for careful monitoring of Indian Ocean conditions.

Coping effectively with the first dry season, in OND, will be critical. This post describes how we can effectively anticipate OND rainfall deficits and OND above-normal OND rains.

**1.4 Using the Indo-Warm Pool Heating Gradient to explore extremes**

By using OND ‘atmospheric heating’, described below, we find relationships that can help describe and anticipate**both**OND droughts and OND flood seasons. The Indo-Warm pool Heating Gradient (IWHG) can help us understand and predict the whipsaw behavior seen in Figure 1. Like recent MAM droughts, OND dry seasons are associated with increased atmospheric heating over the Warm Pool region. OND wet seasons, on the other hand, are linked to exceptionally intense atmospheric heating over the western Indian Ocean. The dry and wet SST drivers link to the WPG and positive IOD/El Niño events. The 1950-2023 OND heating gradient between the western Indian Ocean and the Warm Pool (the IWHG index) tracks exceptionally well with observed eHorn rains (R20.69), and we show below that climate model-based forecasts of the IWHG show surprising skill at long leads (1991-2023 July forecast correlation=0.82). For both negative and positive IWHG cases, July model forecasts can anticipate extreme IWHG states with high levels of certainty (many hits, few misses and false alarms). The IWHG can help us understand the recent whipsaw behavior of the OND rainy season, and IWHG forecasts might contribute to regional East Africam climate forecasting activities.

Current climate model forecasts for OND 2024, based on July model simulations (described below) anticipate yet another dry season. While forecast certainty will increase over the next few months, the time to start planning and preparing for this potential shock is now. In 2023, the climate forecast community did an excellent job of anticipating the very wet rainy season. But more could have been done to prepare for these very heavy rains. Now, in 2024, as in 2010, 2016 and 2020, an emerging La Niña threatens to bring sequential dry seasons during OND and MAM droughts. But in 2024 our ability to understand, anticipate and communicate these shocks has improved greatly.

1. **Data**

To characterize eHorn precipitation we use 1981-2023 CHIRPS observations. We extend eHorn regional precipitation back to 1950 using the high-quality Centennial Trends gridded station data archive[(Funk et al 2015)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Cf2FzR). The Centennial Trends archive ends in 2014, but during their period of overlap (1981-2014), the eHorn OND precipitation averages are highly correlated (R=0.99), and a simple regression (b05mm, b10.95, R2=0.98) is used convert the gridded station data estimates to CHIRPS compatible values. To represent SST we use version 5 of the NOAA Extended Reconstruction archive. Atmospheric conditions are represented by the ERA5 reanalysis. Climate model forecasts of SST are drawn from the North American Multi-Model Ensemble (NMME). Historically, this study uses the multi-model mean of six models: CanCM4i-IC3, COLA-RSMAS-CCSM4, GEM5-NEMO, GFDL-SPEAR, NASA-GEOSS2S, NCEP-CFSv2. For OND 2024, however, the CanCM4i model was dropped, because it showed implausibly cool SST anomalies across the entire Indo-Pacific basin.

1. **Methods**

Following Funk et al. 2023B, we examine ‘Atmospheric Heating’, but focus on the OND season rather than MAM. Our 2023B paper used heating over the Indo-Pacific Warm Pool (15°S-15°N, 90-150°E) to link changes in the strength of the Walker Circulation and warming in the West Pacific to more frequent MAM eHorn dry seasons. Such analyses provide causal explanations for the success of Pacific SST gradient-based forecasts, as discussed in Funk et al. 2023A. Here we expand our analysis to the OND season, include the Indian Ocean region and wet eHorn seasons, and describe how the IWHG can be used in conjunction with climate model SST forecasts to anticipate many extreme OND rainy seasons.

Atmospheric heating represents the total amount of heat energy entering the atmosphere, in Watts per meter squared (Wm-2), over a given region. A major driver of heating is the heat released during precipitation, due to the condensation of water vapor. This heating is augmented by low level heat convergence.

Atmospheric heating is related to changes in the stability of air over the Indian Ocean and East Africa[(Schwarzwald et al 2023)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?zyaALO)and modulation of the Indian Ocean branch of the Walker Circulation. The Walker Circulation describes the equatorial east-west circulation along the Indo-Pacific that contrasts wet rising air over the warm pool with dry sinking air over East Africa and the East Pacific[(Bjerknes 1969)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?CaG96P). As the amount of water vapor increases in a warming atmosphere, increased moisture convergence and precipitation may lead to more extreme atmospheric heating values. When such extremes are located over the western Indian Ocean, the eHorn is likely to experience very heavy rains and flooding, as in 2019 and 2023. On the other hand, when intense heating is located over the Indo-Pacific Warm Pool, the eHorn may experience multi-season droughts, as in 2016/17 and 2020/22.

Atmospheric heating, while more complex, compared to SSTs,  provides a direct measure of the strength of atmospheric forcing. This can be important, because eHorn OND relationships to SST are non-stationary[(Nicholson 2015)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?KFnCti), and many regions of the Indo-Pacific are warming rapidly. Furthermore, heating can provide an evidence-based foundation for recognizing predictable, actionable climate extremes. When Indo-Pacific atmospheric heating becomes very strong, the forcing of teleconnected rainfall extremes (i.e. droughts or floods) will also become extreme. Climate forecasting can be thought of within a ‘signal-to-noise’ setting. When the IWHG is in an extreme state, the role of predictable climate, versus less-predictable weather, will increase. This can, and should, lead to improved proactive interventions. Given the good predictability of the OND rainy season, the opportunities for effective disaster risk reduction are particularly clear. Furthermore, foreknowledge of likely good rainy seasons can be leveraged by farmers to boost crop production, and improve economic outcomes.

The main idea behind the use of atmospheric heating as a descriptive variable is to measure the strength of climate disruptions vis-a-vis teleconnections – i.e. the forcing of terrestrial variations in climate by persistent changes in sea surface temperatures. While these changes are linked to both natural modes of variability such as the El Niño Southern Oscillation and the IOD, as well as human-caused climate change, it can be difficult to assess what a shift in SST might imply in terms of climatic forcing. This is because SST shifts do not directly produce climate disruptions. Rather, warm/cold tropical SST anomalies decrease/increase local low-level air pressure values, which shift winds, patterns of moisture and heat convergence, and precipitation. Heavy precipitation over the tropical oceans releases large amounts of ‘diabatic’ heat, which will typically be augmented by smaller amounts of heating due to the trapping of longwave radiation by water vapor and the advection of heat into the planetary boundary layer. This heating will be further augmented by the convergence of heat. For a given column of air in the atmosphere (Figure 2), we can define atmospheric heating as:

Atm Heating = Diabatic Heating + HeatConvergence              (eq 1)

The terms in equation 1 are measured in Watts per meter squared, Wm-2, and represent the rate at which heat energy is added, or subtracted, from a column of air. Locations with strong atmospheric heating will tend to be unstable and rainy. Areas with atmospheric cooling will tend to be associated with stable conditions without rain.

As the atmosphere warms, it contains more heat, and warmer air tends to hold more water vapor, so it is possible that both diabatic heating and heat convergence extremes may be increasing. This heating will directly drive modulations in the Walker Circulation[(Gill 1980)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?cSdWEl), and modeling and statistical analyses have linked Indian Ocean heating to variations in eastern and southern African precipitation[(Funk et al 2008)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?K58bXT). Our 2023 paper found that increased heating over the Warm Pool area (90-150°E, 15°S-15°N) during March-April-May (MAM) increased the frequency of dry MAM eHorn seasons; linking climate change-related increases in Warm Pool heating to dry long rains outcomes. Here, we extend our analysis to OND, and include an evaluation of atmospheric heating over the western Indian Ocean (50-70°E, 10°S-10°N) – i.e. the western pole of the Indian Ocean Dipole. Strong atmospheric heating over the western Indian Ocean is strongly associated with extreme eHorn OND rains.

To capture both wet and dry extremes, we define the Indo-Warm Pool Heating Gradient (IWHG) as the difference between Western Indian Ocean and Warm Pool atmospheric heating. We also develop SST-based estimates of the IWHG, or IWHGest, based on the IOD, equatorial west Pacific SST (WP, 110-140°E, 10°S-10°N) anomalies and Niño3.4 SST anomalies:

IWHGest= b0 + b1IOD + b2WP + b3Niño34                              (eq 2)

Because of the non-normality of atmospheric heating (which is related to non-normal precipitation), quantile matching was used to improve the performance of our IWHG estimates,

IWHGq,est= IWHG[quantile(IWHGest)]                               (eq 3)

Where IWHG are the sorted 1982-to-2023 ERA IWHG values, and quantile(IWHGest) is the corresponding rank of the 1982-2024 NMME-based IWHGestforecasts.

**4. Results**

**4.1 Asymmetric relationships with SSTs**

We begin by plotting standardized OND SST anomalies for recent dry (1998, 2005,  2010, 2016, 2020, 2021, 2022) and wet (1997, 2002, 2006, 2011, 2015, 2019, 2023)  eHorn seasons (Fig. 3AB). Note that most of the dry seasons (all except 2005) have been associated with La Niña conditions, combined with exceptionally warm equatorial West Pacific (90-140E, 15S-15N) SST. These droughts can be effectively predicted using the WPG[(Hoell and Funk 2013)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?izyf0k), defined as the difference in standardized SST over the equatorial West Pacific and Niño3.4 region in equatorial eastern Pacific. During La Niña seasons, increasingly warm west Pacific SST enhance the strength of the Walker Circulation, increasing dryness over the Horn of Africa[(Hoell and Funk 2014, Funk et al 2018)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?MaonC0). Wet seasons, conversely, tend to be associated with both positive Indian Ocean Dipole and El Niño events (1997, 2006, 2023), very strong positive IOD (pIOD) events (2019), or El Niño events (2002, 2015). The Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) is measured using the difference between western Indian Ocean (50-70°E, 15S-15°N) and eastern Indian Ocean (90-110°E, 10°S-0) SST.

The large standardized anomalies in Fig. 3A and 3B imply that dry and wet eHorn events are highly predictable – given that they have clear SST signatures. These signatures, furthermore, are clearly linked to well-understood climate modes. It should be noted, however, that the structures in 3A and 3B are asymmetric. Fig. 2A emphasizes that most recent droughts are linked to the WPG, and arise due to influence of La Niña, augmented by human induced warming in the equatorial west Pacific. Multi-year La Niña combined persistent exceptional warmth in the west Pacific can lead to devastating multi-season droughts as in 2020-2022 (Fig. 1A). Negative IOD conditions in 2005, 2010, 2016, and 2021 and 2022 also contributed to drought, and negative IOD conditions often co-occur with La Niña events. Figure 3B suggests that wet seasons, on the other hand, are largely driven by very warm western Indian Ocean SST.

**4.2 Forecasts of 2024 IOD and WPG values**

A time series plot of 1950-2024 IOD and WPG values (Fig. 3C) helps us understand drivers between recent eHorn whipsaw OND seasons. In addition to observations, this time series also shows July NMME IOD and WPG forecasts, along with 80th percentile confidence bounds, based on the 1991-2023 forecast standard errors.

The NMME forecast estimates for OND indicate a very negative WPG of -1.9 standardized anomalies. In 1998, 2016, 2017, 2020, 2021 and 2022 strong negative WPG values were observed, and these conditions were accompanied by dry OND seasons in every year except 2017. The WPG forecast for 2024 (-1.9Z±0.6Z) is similar to these seasons. The increase in the frequency of very negative WPG values is related to human-induced warming in the western Pacific, and the frequency of strong negative WPG events is expected to increase[(Funk et al 2023b)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ASSVvB).

In 1997, 2006, 2019 and 2023, we see exceptionally strong pIOD events, largely driven by exceptionally warm western Indian Ocean SST.This region is also warming rapidly due to climate change, and more frequent pIOD events are also expected to occur[(Cai et al 2014)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?eiIPlP). The IOD forecast for 2024 is -0.4±0.4 C. This suggests moderate negative IOD conditions similar to 2016, 2021 and 2022. Negative IOD conditions often arise during La Niña events. The 80th percentile confidence interval suggests, however, that the event could fade or strengthen substantially.

**4.3 relationships with ERA5 atmospheric heating**

Building on our analysis of MAM droughts (Funk et al. 2023B), we plot atmospheric heating composites for recent wet and dry seasons (Fig. 4A). Dry seasons are characterized by strong positive heating anomalies over the Indo-Pacific Warm Pool region and negative anomalies over the western Indian Ocean and central Pacific. These responses are characteristic of a stronger Walker Circulation. Atmospheric moisture transports (shown with arrows) indicate strong eastward zonal anomalies over the equatorial Indian Ocean, consistent with the strong teleconnection between equatorial zonal winds over the central Indian Ocean[(Hastenrath et al 2011, 2007, Nicholson 2015)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?kIbf8U). The wet season composites are close to an inversion of the dry season anomalies. This means that unlike SSTs, the relationships are symmetric.

Increased atmospheric heating over the western Indian Ocean, less heating over the Warm Pool, and westward moisture transports over the equatorial Indian Ocean are associated with increased rainfall over the eastern Horn.

To produce a single index describing these relationships, we calculate the ‘**I**ndo-**W**arm Pool**H**eating**G**radient’ (IWHG). The IWHG measures the difference between heating over the western IOD box (50-70°E, 10°S-10°N) and heating over the Indo-Pacific Warm Pool (90-150°E, 15°S-15°N). The IWHG integrates influences from the IOD and WPG, while also potentially incorporating influences from the MJO and weather extremes.

A scatterplot of 1950-2023 OND IWHG and eHorn SPI values (Fig. 4B) shows that the IWHG tracks very closely with eHorn rains. Wet and dry eHorn seasons are identified as those with SPI values of less than -0.44 or +0.44Z, which corresponds to the bottom and top tercile ranges (i.e. a one-on-three year dry season). The 1950-2023 R2value is 0.69, with a 100 Wm-2change in the gradient associated with a ~0.3Z change in SPI. Visual interpretation of these results led to the selection of threshold gradient values of -100 and +200 Wm-2plotted in Fig. 4B.

IWHG values beyond these extremes do an excellent job of isolating dry and wet seasons. When the IWHG was less than -100 Wm-2, 22 out 33 seasons (73%) experienced below-normal rains.  When the IWHG was greater than +200 Wm-2, 14 out 17 (82%) seasons experienced above-normal rains. Extreme negative IWHG seasons (1975, 1998, 2010) correspond to very strong La Niña years which were also strong negative IOD seasons (-0.6, -0.7, -0.8C). The eHorn SPI in these seasons was -1.4, -1.2, and -1.5Z, respectively. Extreme positive IWHG values occurred in 1997, 2006 and 2019, pIOD seasons with very warm western Indian Ocean SST. Thus, the IWHG incorporates influences from both ENSO and IOD.

**4.4 Evaluating NMME forecasts of the IWHG**

We next explore IWHG predictions, using 1982-2023 NMME SST forecasts for the IOD regions, the equatorial West Pacific SST anomalies, and Niño3.4 SST anomalies (eq. 2).

This approach is similar to using the IOD and WPG, but allows us to assess the relative contribution of western and eastern Pacific SST. To produce these estimates we use a fixed empirical regression between 1982-2023 observed ERA5 IWHG values and observed IOD, western Pacific and Niño3.4 SST anomalies. The regression parameters are b0=12, bIOD=323, bWPAC=-193, and bNINO3=94, with an R2of 0.84 and 38 d.f. The slope parameters have units of Wm-2per °C. The 1983-2023 standard deviations of the IOD, western Pacific and Niño3.4 SST are 0.5, 0.4, and 1°C, respectively, suggesting relative influences of around 160, 77, and 94 Wm-2for the IOD,  WP, and Niño3.4. Note, however, that all of these predictors are correlated with each other. While the influence of the west Pacific is relatively modest, this region warmed +0.5°C between 1982-1991 and 2014-2023, leading to ~-100 Wm-2decreases in the IWHG. This helps set the stage for frequent, but predictable OND dry seasons.

Multi-model NMME forecasts from April to October (lead 6 to lead 0) were evaluated and used to produce IWHG forecasts (IWHGq,est). Forecast SST values were translated into IWHG forecasts using the regression equation shown in eq. 2. While forecasts were produced over the 1982-2023 period, we base our evaluation on the 1991-2023 period. Over time, ocean observations, particularly the density of ocean buoys, has increased, likely leading to improvements in forecast performance. We are also most interested in assessing performance under near-present conditions, i.e. those relevant to an applied forecasting context. To optimize performance in this setting, IWHGq,est valueswere quantile-matched to the observed IWHG distribution; this reduced the influence of an identified bias in the forecasts (eq. 3).

Table 1 displays the corresponding correlation and standard errors from forecasts made in April to October, as well as the statistics based on OND SST. The 1991-2023 correlations for the IWHGq,estforecasts and the observed OND IWHG (R GR) ranged from 0.6 in April, to 0.92 in September and October. In critical forecast months (July and August), the correlations were 0.82 and 0.84, with standard errors of 179 and 121 Wm-2. The observed 1991-2023 IWHG standard deviation was 298 Wm-2. The correlation between the predicted IWHGq,estvalues and observed eHorn OND SPI (R SPI) ranged from 0.55 to 0.93.

Our main question here is ‘*how well can the NMME anticipate the arrival of extreme IWHG seasons*’? In the 33 OND seasons between 1991 and 2023, there were 14 strong negative IWHG seasons (IWHG < -100 Wm-2) and 8 strong positive IWHG seasons (IWHG > +200 Wm-2). In July, the IWHGq,estcorrectly anticipated 11 negative outcomes (1995, 1996, 1998, 1999,  2000, 2005, 2010, 2016, 2020, 2021 and 2022). The forecast missed  2001, 2008 and 2017. 1992, 2007, 2013 were false alarms, with forecast values  ranging from -130 to -180 Wm-2. Thus, when boreal summer forecasts indicate that we are likely to be in  the IWHG < -100 Wm-2danger zone, these forecasts are quite reliable. The July 2024 IWHG forecast (Fig. 4B) indicates that we are quite likely to see an IWHG value of less than -100 Wm-2in OND 2024.

In July, the IWHGq,estcorrectly anticipated five out of eight strong heating events, with the hit rate rising to six and then seven in August and September, likely due to the increased accuracy of Indian Ocean SST conditions[(Kolstad and MacLeod 2022)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?vOyLJa). While not all extremes are captured, the forecasts can capture quite well when above-normal rains and strong positive IWHG values are likely.

**4.5 Spatial maps of IWHG influences and Spearman rank correlations**

We can assess the spatial influence of the IWHG using 1982-2023 by-pixel linear regressions with CHIRPS data (Figure 5). Panels 5A and 5B show that influence of the  IWHG extends across all of Somalia, southern Ethiopia, and most of Kenya and Tanzania. The regression slopes are highest (~ 0.6 mm/Wm-2) in coastal Kenya and Tanzania, southern and central Somalia, and the central highlands of Kenya. A 200 Wm-2IWHG anomaly would produce a 120 mm shift in rainfall. For comparison, the 1991-2023 median and standard deviation of areal eHorn rainfall averages were 150 and 97 mm.

These regressions make it easy to generate scenarios for moderate and strong IWHG extremes (Fig. 5C). Moderate negative IWHG conditions (-200 Wm-2) arose in OND of 2020/2021 and 2022, and were associated with moderate reductions in rainfall. Similar or drier outcomes appear likely in 2024. The impact of these ‘moderate’ droughts, however, were severe in arid and semi-arid lands, where high temperatures and atmospheric evaporative demand led to the rapid degradation of rangelands and poor crop outcomes. Strong positive IWHG values are characteristic of seasons like 1997, 2019 and 2023, which had IWHG values of 595, 579 and 467 Wm-2, respectively.****

While their small sample size makes it difficult to assess forecast skill for the most extreme negative (i.e. 1998 and 2010) or positive (1997, 2019 and 2023) IWHG seasons, July forecasts for these events performed well – reinforcing how IWHG  forecasts can help anticipate both the most extreme droughts and most extreme rainy season months in advance.

Figure 6 displays maps of Spearman rank correlations with IWHG forecast and NMME precipitation forecast at seven lead times, beginning in April and ending in October. For OND, NMME precipitation forecasts are known to exhibit high levels of skill[(Shukla et al 2019)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?VQU3HL) and perform well for the eastern Horn SPI time series (Table 1, R eHorn NMME P).  The NMME precipitation correlation maps in Fig. 6 indicate that this strong performance is spatially focused, with highest skill in southern Somalia and in portions of southeastern Ethiopia and northeastern Kenya. September and October correlations are very good in these areas. In comparison, good IWHG correlations extend further, across Kenya, southwestern Ethiopia,  and Tanzania. TheIWHG forecasts attain surprisingly strong correlations over wider areas, even at longer lead times. This skill is related to skillful NMME SST forecasts, and the fact that IWHG estimates perform well at long lead times (Table 1).

1. **Conclusion – The need to prepare now for the upcoming drought**

Droughts are complex slow-onset disasters. But increasingly sophisticated drought early warning systems can help manage drought risk. Drought risk management involves three pillars: **1.** drought early warning, **2.** drought vulnerability and risk assessment, and **3.** drought preparedness, mitigation, and response[(Tadesse 2016)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?cFT7tJ). Our hope is that advances in early warning can lead to improved drought preparedness, mitigation, and response.

In the eHorn, droughts produce dangerous complex, cascading and interconnected risks. In 2010, 2016, 2020, 2021 and 2022, La Niña conditions augmented by warm west Pacific SSTs helped set the stage for dry OND rainy seasons. In 2010, 2016, 2021 and 2022 negative IOD conditions further elevated risks. A similar trifecta of forcing seems likely in OND of 2024. In 2010, 2016, 2020, 2021 and 2022 droughts decimated livestock populations and reduced crop production. Elevated food prices and reduced terms-of-trade for livestock posed severe challenges for vulnerable households. In OND 2024, a similar drought shock appears likely – but appropriate planning may help blunt the impact of poor rains and high temperatures.
