# Pamoja Ni Bora: Together is Better When Forecasting in Kenya

**Introduction**

You have likely heard of the African proverb, ‘to go fast, go alone, to go far, go together’. In Swahili, pamoja ni bora means ‘together is better’. Together can describe effective team work at the Kenyan National Meteorological Services Authority (KMSA), University of California’s Climate Hazards Center (CHC) and the Famine Early Warning Systems Network (FEWS NET), the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT), Rhiza Research, and the University Corporation for Atmospheric Research’s ‘Innovative Capacity Development Program’ ([ICDP](https://www.comet.ucar.edu/capacity-development)), or it can mean all  six of these groups working together to advance early warning science and sub-seasonal forecasting in Kenya (Figure 1). Here, we describe how this unique collaboration is making a difference in Kenya, and how it provides a good model for respectful, effective and sustainable advances in African weather forecasting systems, at authoritative institutions like KMSA.

![Figure 1. From Dr. Okello’s GHACOF72 presentation ‘How developing National Integrated Observations-Forecast Systems can build community resilience’, January 27 2026, Nairobi Kenya.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Figure1-300x170.jpg)

**In 2023, better together at seasonal time scales**

In 2023, supported by FEWS NET, the CHC started close collaborations with KMSA then KMD, focusing on two objectives: the production, installation and maintenance of 3D Printed Automatic Weather stations (3D PAWS), and the improvement of Kenyan climate services. Fifty-four 3D PAWS stations have been installed in Kenya ([blog](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1840)), greatly improving the monitoring of extreme events, such as the terrible 2025 short rainy season drought ([blog](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1905)). Similar efforts are extending into Southern Africa ([press release](https://fews.net/fews-net-initiative-enhances-local-weather-monitoring-southern-africa)), Ethiopia and Somalia. In terms of climate services, CHC-KMSA collaboration in 2023 helped anticipate the very extreme short rainy season rains ([blog](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1315)), and an early start of the rainy season – with good prospects of bountiful rainfall for farmers ([blog](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1327)). Working with CIAT-Alliance Bioversity and iShamba, the team released videos ([video1](https://www.facebook.com/ShambaShapeUp/videos/1544982102703754/), [video2](https://www.facebook.com/ShambaShapeUp/videos/861892211841771/)) showing farmers how to prepare for and harvest the expected heavy rains. These efforts were prescient and effective, but extreme persistent rains and flooding in Kenya in late 2023 and early 2024 pointed towards a gap – a lack of effective sub-seasonal rainfall forecasts.

**Enter KIT’s International Research Award and the Gates-funded Nimbus Project**

In late 2024, KIT and the SCHROFF Foundation [provided](https://www.kit.edu/kit/english/pi_2024_092_2024-international-excellence-award-of-kit-goes-to-climatologist-chris-funk.php) an International Research Award to support the collaboration of Chris Funk (CHC) ([looKIT](https://www.km.kit.edu/english/archive-lookit_11343.php), p. 54), KIT tropical meteorologists led by Professor Andreas Fink, and scientists from KMSA. In May of 2025, the award supported a KIT visit by two leading KMSA meteorologists: Mary Kilavi and Fredrick Sedah. This allowed the collaboration to begin in an uncommon way. The professors Fink and Funk didn’t talk very much; they listened. What they heard was that KMSA had quite a substantial gap in terms of sub-seasonal and monthly temperature and rainfall forecasts. KMSA has a good weekly weather forecasting system, and a good seasonal climate forecasting process, but relatively few information products at in-between time-scales. KIT scientists have deep experience with the ECMWF sub-seasonal forecast system. The FEWS NET-funded CHC team has lots of experience down-scaling model forecasts ([paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-022-01468-2)), developing sub-seasonal monitoring systems ([page](https://chc.ucsb.edu/monitoring/subx)), and using these forecasts to improve crop forecasts ([paper](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/2752-5295/adcadf/meta)) and rainy season onset predictions. The [Rhiza](https://rhizaresearch.org/)-led Nimbus consortium brings experts in AI forecasting to the mix.

As [described](https://climate.uchicago.edu/news/initiative-brings-a-climate-adaptation-tool-to-communities-globally-weather-forecasts-guided-by-human-needs-and-the-power-of-ai/#:~:text=%E2%80%9CWeather%20forecasts%20optimized%20for%20people's,Change%2C%20Food%20Security%20and%20Innovation.) by Nobel award-winning economist, Michael Kremer and [exemplified](https://hannahritchie.substack.com/p/artificial-intelligence-could-dramatically) by AI-driven monsoon onset forecasts in India, “*weather forecasts optimized for people’s specific needs have an incredible return on investment*”. In Africa, Nimbus AI-forecasters from the Turing Institute, University of Chicago, University of Leeds and the University of California, Santa Barbara are quantifying the benefits of AI enhancements in Kenya, Ghana and Senegal. A new WRI [paper](https://www.wri.org/research/climate-adaptation-investment-case) ([summary](https://www.wri.org/news/release-wri-study-finds-climate-adaptation-investments-yield-massive-returns)) describes how investments in forecasting can reveal massive benefits, with a $1 dollar USD expenditure in climate adaptation producing a 10-fold return on investment.

Forecast-based early action can reduce impacts ([paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048969723041293)) while also seizing opportunities associated with beneficial weather conditions ([paper](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2023EF003524)). To be truly effective, however, such efforts should be “*underpinned by capacity development, knowledge management; and partnerships of co-production, communication and coordination*” ([paper)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-46742-6). Modeling impacts and prioritizing user needs helps make forecasts actionable, and acted upon ([paper](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2024EF005799)). From this orientation, KMSA scientists are the first and most important set of ‘users’, and the first and most important set of ‘producers’. Collaboration with international partners, and the infusion of new technologies, should enhance, not supplant, the forecast products being produced by KMSA. These co-developed products, in turn, should help inform KMSA advisories and alerts.

**Exciting co-developments at KIT**

The KIT International Research Award has supported KIT graduate students, Alexander Jong and Jannis Dahlem, and an extended research residency for Dr. [Phillip Okello Ochieng](https://www.linkedin.com/in/dr-phillip-okello-ochieng-phd-219992a2/), a KMSA forecast specialist. Over the past year, Alec, Jannis and Phillip have worked closely with  Professor Andreas Fink, Dr. Simon Ageet and Yiping Yu from KIT, Mary Kilavi, Hannah Wangari and Fredrick Sedah from KMSA, and Chris Funk and Genevieve Flaspohler from the CHC and Rhiza. This team identified the latest ‘Cycle 49’ ECMWF sub-seasonal (1 to 45 day) forecasts as an ideal starting point, given rapid (daily) updating, relatively easy automated access via the ECMWF data server, and an established reputation for good performance over East Africa.

Initial efforts at KIT focused on assessing the skill of the ECMWF forecasts, which exhibited reasonable, but decreasing skill over week 1, 2 and 3 over Kenya. These results were presented at an invited talk at the 72nd Greater Horn of Africa Climate Outlook Forum by Dr. Okello (Figure 2). Over time, the accuracy of the forecasts diminished, as expected, but overall correlations were promising. In parallel, research at the CHC focused on developing code to use ‘quantile matching’ to increase the resolution of the forecasts to a very high level – 0.05° – using CHIRPS3 gridded rainfall observations ([blog](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1510)). Finally, the KIT added a third new and exciting component to the system – the innovative use of GitHub [actions](https://github.com/features/actions) running co-developed python routines. This lightweight system (available [here](https://github.com/alecjong-lab/ECMWF-S2S4AFRICA)), updates daily, providing week 1, 2, 3 and 4 rainfall forecasts, along with station and satellite-based rainfall observations. Soon, this system will be run by Dr. Okello, and other scientists at KMSA. KMSA can modify the code as they see fit, and we anticipate that more forecast models and more improvements will be added soon.

![Figure 2. Correlation between 2005-to-2024 weekly ECMWF forecasts and IMERG data. From Dr. Okello’s GHACOF72 presentation ‘How developing National Integrated Observations-Forecast Systems can build community resilience’, January 27 2026, Nairobi Kenya.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Figure2-300x226.jpg)

This put KMSA in the driver’s seat, as appropriate. KMSA is the authorized provider of Kenyan weather predictions. But the international team is also providing an engine for growth, a muscle car driven by innovation.

**Correctly Anticipating Early Season Rains in March**

While still under development, the ECMWF forecast system is being used by KMSA and FEWS NET to help track extreme weather. The CHC, of course, is also using its own multi-model sub-seasonal forecast system ([page](https://chc.ucsb.edu/monitoring/subx)). The collaboration team has also been holding routine meetings to discuss results and weather outlooks. These forecasts and meetings helped KMSA anticipate and communicate risks and opportunities associated with surprising extreme rains in late February and early March. This time period, at the very beginning of the March-to-May ‘long’ rains, is typically dry. Late February rainfall observations, and early March rainfall forecasts, however, were very wet. Despite being surprised by the rapid onset of the 2026 long rains, the collaboration team was able to quickly analyze the forecasts, discuss the results internally, and post a blog describing the results on March 2nd ([blog](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1923)).

Figure 3 of this post presented a forecast for March, averaged over the entire country. With CHIRPS3 preliminary observations now in hand, we can examine an update of this scatter plot (Figure 3). With an R2 of 0.7, the downscaled forecasts do a very good job of estimating monthly totals. Between 2005 and 2026, only one March was wetter (2018), likely due to the influence of the Madden-Julian oscillation ([paper](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4433/9/12/472)). This forecast was generated on February 28th. When averaged over the entire country, the downscaled ECMWF forecast for Kenya performed even better (Figure 4), with an R2 value of 0.86. The predicted national average, 60 mm, underpredicted the observed value of 86 mm – but certainly did a great job of anticipating extreme rains.

Figure 5 presents these data as maps. While there are certainly differences, the forecast called for rainfall across almost the entire country, and this was largely the case. Within FEWS NET, 25 mm of rain over a ten-day period is often used to identify a potential start of season. In 2026, CHIRPS3 preliminary data suggest that an exceptional fraction of the country (58%) met this criteria, the rainfall predictions indicated 62%. Using the 25 mm threshold, we can categorize each year’s March 1-10 forecast as a collection of ‘hits’, ’misses’ and ‘false alarms’. Hits occur when both the forecast and observations were greater than or equal to 25 mm. Misses happen when the observations, but not the forecasts, met the 25 mm threshold. False alarms represent the opposite case.

![Figure 5. Predicted (left) and observed (right) March 1-to-10 rainfall totals. Predictions were those presented in this blog post, based on downscaled ECMWF forecasts. Observations are based on CHIRPS3 preliminary observations – which will change as more observations are incorporated.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Figure5-300x209.png)

Figure 6 shows a time series of hits, misses and false alarms, for March 1-10 outcomes greater than or equal to 25 mm, for Kenya. Over the past 22 years, the results are compelling. The observed number of wet pixels, with rainfall >= 25 mm, corresponds to the sum of the orange and blue bars. In most years, these totals match fairly closely with the forecasts, and in the very wet events in 2010, 2018 and 2026, the number of misses are low. Infrequent extremely wet forecasts, when they occur, appear to be associated with few misses, and hence appear reliable based on this data. The same does not appear to be true when it comes to misses in the moderately wet events that occurred in 2016 and 2020. These years had a lot of misses. This type of validation results are fairly reassuring. The forecast system captures wet events well, and when such forecasts occur, false alarm rates are low, even at a very high 0.05° resolution.

![Figure 6. A barplot of 2005 to 2026 25mm threshold hits, misses and false alarms. Based on 0.05° downscaled ECMWF forecasts and preliminary CHIRPS3 observations. False alarm counts have been inverted. The sum of hits and misses equals the total number of observed pixels in each year with observed rainfall of 25 mm or more.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Figure6-300x266.png)

Historically, FEWS NET defines ‘Start of Seasons’ defined from an agricultural perspective as a ten-day period with 25 mm or more of rainfall, followed by a ~20 day period with at least 20 mm of rainfall, in total. Validation results for the Start of Season forecast made in early March ([blog](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1923)) are very similar to those made for the March 1-10 rainfall totals (Figure 7).

![Figure 7. A barplot of 2005 to 2026 March 1-10 ‘Start of Season’ hits, misses and false alarms. **Collaboration Impacts and Going Further Together**

While this system is still under development, and will certainly continue to improve as enhanced forecasts and downscaling techniques are evaluated and entrained, the forecasts and dialogs in February had a positive impact on KMSA advisories and alerts. KMSA incorporates a rich set of information from many sources; weekly meetings with KMSA/Rhiza/KIT/CHC team members helped identify and anticipate the exceptionally early and heavy rains in early March. The S2S forecasting system and discussions helped inform KMSA rainy season onset forecasts and the related advisory ([here](https://meteo.go.ke/documents/2706/MAM_2026_Season_forecast_Onset_Update.pdf)), which found that the “rainfall that is currently being experienced is likely to continue into early March 2026 and is expected to meet the onset criteria over several regions.” These results also helped support a series of extreme rain alerts issued by KMD ([here](https://meteo.go.ke/weather-warnings/)) on February 20th, February 25th, March 3rd and March 18th.

Going forward, our collaboration is in a good position to sustainably support improvements in forecasting, at KMSA and by KMSA. The GitHub architecture supports this in two ways. First, by conception and construction, GitHub is designed to support collaborative coding. Secondly, the GitHub action framework provides an easy to setup and maintain framework for storing moderate amounts of data and automating processes. Collaborating scientists can add to the code base. In terms of forecasting, we envision adding more forecast models, more forecast variables, more merging and multivariate estimation techniques, and more choices for downscaling. Though this list may just be the beginning. As new models become available, AI or otherwise, the system developed at KIT with KMSA should be able incorporate new inputs, bringing cutting edge developments into the hands of Kenyan forecast experts.
