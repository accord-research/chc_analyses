# Latest forecasts indicate an exceptionally intense IOD event and East African Rains

**Chris Funk and****Laura Harrison**

**Overview**

This post focuses on the latest North American Multi-Model Ensemble (NMME) Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) forecasts for October-November-December (OND). For the latest El Niño3.4 forecast, see this link. We also provide a related forecast for the Eastern East African September-October-November-December rains (SOND). This region includes Ethiopia, Kenya and Somalia east and south of 8°N, 36°E.

**Observations and the latest forecasts indicate an exceptional positive IOD event – perhaps the strongest event on record**

Since[July](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1272), NMME forecasts have indicated the likely development of a strong positive IOD event. These events are associated with warmer-than-usual sea surface temperatures (SST) in the western Indian Ocean and cooler-than-normal temperatures in the eastern equatorial Indian Ocean. Such conditions have been associated with extreme rains and flooding over East Africa and drought and fire over Australia. Strong El Nino conditions tend to co-occur and amplify positive IOD influences. Very warm sea surface temperatures in the Nino3.4 region indicate this El Nino is already a strong event ([blog](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1338)).  Importantly, convergence is coming from recent ocean temperature observations and model forecasts that both very strong IOD and strong El Nino conditions are very likely to be present and impactful to eastern East Africa’s OND 2023 rainfall season. Our recent CHC[post](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1338)describes why strong OND El Nino conditions are likely.

El Niño is a coupled ocean-atmosphere phenomenon. Its characteristics are[closely monitored](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/enso-detection-and-monitoring-depends-data)using a variety of indicators – surface and subsurface ocean characteristics and air pressure and winds at multiple heights – to ascertain if telltale signs of El Nino are present and how these may evolve. One of the key indicators used to measure event strength is the degree by which sea surface temperatures rise above typically cooler levels in the equatorial central-eastern tropical Pacific Ocean (the Nino 3.4 region).[NOAA’s probabilistic strength forecasts](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso_advisory/strengths/index.php), for example, define strong events as those having 3-month Nino 3.4 SST anomalies that are greater than +1.5 °C.

Between July and the first week of October, the strength of the IOD increased dramatically, as shown by a time-series of the Indian Ocean Dipole strength, obtained from the Australian Bureau of Meteorology (Figure 1). Current conditions are very similar to the peak values obtained during the last extreme IOD event in 2019 (FEWS NET[report](https://fews.net/east-africa/special-report/january-2020)).

![Figure 1. A Weekly IOD Index Time Series plot obtained from the Australian Bureau of Meteorology.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/BOM_IOD_Timeseries.png)

The latest NMME IOD forecasts (Figure 2) indicate a substantial increase in the intensity of the IOD, when compared to NMME forecasts from September (see Figure 1[here](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1315)). While the western Indian Ocean forecast (Figure 2A) continues to be exceptionally warm (similar to the 2015 forecast), the eastern Indian Ocean forecast has cooled substantially, with forecasts dropping from ~-0.9Z to -1.9Z (Figure 2B).  This was a robust forecast indicated by 5 out of the six NMME models. The individual eastern Indian Ocean model forecasts from the CanCM4i-IC3, COLA-RSMAS-CCSM4, GFDL-SPEAR, NASA-GEOSS2S and NCEP-CFSv2 ranged from -1.3Z to -2.4Z. The GEM5-NEMO model continued to anticipate moderately cool eastern Indian Ocean temperatures (~-0.6Z). Maps from NOAA PSD show that early October eastern Indian Ocean sea surface temperatures are[below normal](https://www.psl.noaa.gov/map/clim/sst.shtml). Subsurface temperature anomalies beneath the eastern equatorial Indian Ocean, as represented by the NCEP Global Ocean Data Assimilation System ([GODAS](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/GODAS/pentad.shtml)), are very striking (Figure 3). Between 70°E and 90°E, an equatorial longitude-by-depth anomaly plot identifies anomalies of**less than -5**°**C**. These observations, as well as the known reinforcing influence of strong El Niño conditions, support the ensemble forecast’s expectation of very cool eastern Indian Ocean OND SSTs, with conditions similar to 2019.

Expectations for a very cool eastern Indian Ocean and a very warm western Indian Ocean leads to a very exceptional positive IOD forecast (Figure 2C). The forecast, in fact, is the strongest on record (+2.9Z). The lower-bound of the 90th confidence interval for this forecast (noted with a vertical black line), suggests that there is a 90% chance of an extremely strong positive IOD event, similar in magnitude to 1997 and 2019. This outlook seems plausible given the current very warm conditions in the western Indian Ocean, the large subsurface temperature anomalies in the eastern Indian Ocean (Figure 3), the fairly robust forecast for very warm eastern Indian Ocean temperatures (Figure 2B), and the observed strong El Niño conditions in early October  ([blog](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1338)). Time-series of the eastern, western and IOD NMME forecasts (Figure 4) emphasize similarities with 1997 and 2019.

**Regression-based forecasts for Eastern East Africa SOND rainfall**

In the past, we have used regressions based on IOD and Niño3.4 SSTs to predict SOND or OND East African Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) values. We repeat that process here, beginning in 1996 (as in prior posts), because[prior research](https://rmets.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/joc.4259)has identified non-stationary relationships. At this short lead time, 1996-2022 NMME IOD forecasts are very-well correlated with observed SOND rainfall totals (R=0.92), and the correlation with NMME Niño3.4 forecasts is substantially lower (R=0.6).  A regression using NMME IOD and Niño3.4 forecasts, has an R2of 0.86, and a standard error 0.4Z. According to this forecast, there is a 90% chance of very heavy rains (SPI > 2.5Z), with rainfall totals similar to 1997 or 2019.
