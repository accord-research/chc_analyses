# September NMME IOD and El Niño forecasts indicate extreme conditions for late 2023 and early 2024

Chris Funk, Gideon Galu, Diriba Korecha, Absae Sedah, Laura Harrison, Tamuka Magadzire, Shraddhanand Shukla, Donghoon Lee, Frank Davenport, Pete Peterson, Will Turner, and Gregory Husak

**Introduction**

This post updates our Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) and El Niño forecasts using September climate model predictions. Because the forecasts anticipate both extreme western Indian Ocean and eastern equatorial Pacific sea surface temperatures (SST), we provide a more detailed explanation of how the CHC combines forecasts from multiple models. The advantage of this skill-weighted approach is that it provides detailed empirical estimates of uncertainty. We use these assessments to show that there is a very high probability that we face both a strong IOD and El Niño in the coming months. Our post then concludes with composite analyses of seasonal rainfall outcomes, and a brief description of Somali crop and streamflow outcomes. For a more detailed discussion of Kenyan Crop Water Requirements, we have prepared a separate analysis[here](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1xBHjrghSrEqeOsoqUcOkv1e7VUtb-kcZwA1QoRqw41M/edit).

**The CHC NMME Combination Methodology**

Given the importance of the forecasts presented here, we briefly describe the CHC forecast methodology. The CHC’s general approach to climate forecasting combines a strong focus on empirical analysis with SST forecasts from the latest dynamical climate model prediction systems. This approach integrates information from the observational record and information from the latest model forecasts. This strategy performs well, and also provides precise assessments of uncertainty.

To develop our forecasts of SST indices, we begin by defining target regions. Here we use the Western Indian Ocean (10°S-10°N, 50°E-70°E, WIO), Eastern Indian Ocean (10°S-0°N, 90°E-110°E, EIO) and Niño3.4 region in the eastern equatorial Pacific (5°S-5°N, 170°E-120°W), due to their representation of the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) and the El Niño-Southern Oscillation. These large-scale climate modes are important drivers of rainfall variability in East Africa.

The CHC estimation process involves creating a skill-weighted combination of multi-model SST forecasts and providing an empirical basis for assessing forecast skill. For a given season, we extract SST forecasts from six different climate models for each ocean region. These model forecasts are drawn from the North American Multi-Model Ensemble (NMME), accessed from the IRI data library. These time-series are averaged across each per-model ensemble and standardized based on a 1982-2022 baseline. A standardized time-series of observed SST, based on the NOAA Extended Reconstructed SST version 5 data set, is also calculated. A separate regression model is developed to relate each NMME time-series to the observations. The corresponding R2values are then used to create a multi-model weighted average of forecast SST. The purpose is to provide more weight to predictions coming from models that historically made skillful predictions. A simple bivariate regression then relates this composite with the observed data.

Table 1 provides summary statistics based on early September NMME forecasts of September-October-November-December (SOND) Indian Ocean SST values. In addition to the R2values and SOND 2023 standardized SST forecasts, we also provide counts for the number of years when forecasts exceeded (i.e. were higher or lower than) the 2023 forecast. In this post ‘Z’ refers to standardized anomalies, based on a 1982-2022 baseline. Table 2 provides similar results for Niño3.4 SST forecasts, for SOND and November-December-January-February (NDJF).

**Results 1. By-Model NMME Forecast Results**

**WIO SOND 2023**: For the Western Indian Ocean, the September forecasts indicate a very high level of convergence that indicates exceptionally warm SOND SST(Table 1). All the models have high R2values (> 0.71) and the combined model R2is substantially better (0.84). All of the models predict  very warm WIO conditions (SSThigher than +2.0Z). Five models predict such a warm WIO SST for SOND 2023 that only one previous season in the 1982-2022 hindcast period was predicted to be warmer (SOND 2015). One model (GEOSS2S) predicts the 2023 forecast of +3Z to be the warmest ever. Thus, the SOND 2023 multi-model ensemble forecast is incredibly warm for the WIO (+2.4 Z), and just a little cooler than the 2015 forecast.

**EIO SOND 2023**: For the Eastern Indian Ocean, the September forecasts indicate a moderate level of skill and lower level of convergence (Table 1). The individual model R2values range from 0.5 to 0.66, and the ensemble forecast performs substantially better, with an R2value of 0.75. The worst performing model, NCEP-CFSv2, indicates a positive 2023 anomaly. The other models, and the ensemble average indicate moderately cool eastern Indian Ocean conditions (-0.7 Z).

**Niño3.4 SOND 2023**: For the Niño3.4 region, the September forecasts indicate a very high level of convergence that indicates very warm SOND El Niño conditions (Table 2). All the models have high R2values (>0.66) and the combined model R2is 0.89. The individual model forecasts all indicate strong El Niño conditions, with standardized anomalies of > +1.4Z, and the ensemble average forecast is substantially warmer (+1.7Z). Given that the observed Niño3.4 1982-2022 standard deviation is 1.1°C and that the 1991-2020 standardized anomaly mean is -0.5Z, a +1.4Z and +1.7Z standardized anomaly would be equivalent to a +2.0°C and a +2.4°C anomaly, assuming a WMO standard 1991-2020 baseline. In other words, the models provide a robust indication that we should expect a strong El Niño in SOND.

**Niño3.4 NDJF 2023/24**: For the NDJF season, the September forecasts also indicate a very high level of convergence that indicates very warm SOND El Niño conditions (Table 2). All the models have high R2values (> 0.66) and the combined model R2is 0.88. All of the models predict  SSTs that indicate strong El Niño conditions, with standardized anomalies of > +1.3Z. The ensemble average forecast is substantially warmer (+1.6Z). Given that the observed Niño3.4 1982-2022 NDJF standard deviation is +1.1°C and that the 1991-2020 NDJF standardized anomaly mean is -0.5Z, a +1.3Z and +1.6Z standardized anomaly would be equivalent to a +1.9°C and a +2.3°C anomaly, assuming a WMO standard 1991-2020 baseline. In other words, the models provide a robust indication that we should expect a strong El Niño in NDJF.

**Results 2. Examining potential East Africa Climate Analogs for SOND 2023**

The Indian Ocean and Niño3.4 results are presented as scatterplots (Figure 1 and Figure 2). The large red dots in these figures indicate the 2023 forecast, with the observed y-axis value assumed to be forecast value. The small red dots and horizontal line indicate the 80th percentile confidence interval of the forecasts. To aid in visualization of our results, the forecast values are color-coded according to  below-normal, normal and above-normal SOND rainy season outcomes for eastern East Africa (Kenya, Somalia, and Ethiopia south of 8°N and east of 38°E). These forecasts indicate that there is an extremely high probability of an exceptionally warm western Indian Ocean and strong positive Indian Ocean Dipole event (Figure 1) accompanied by a strong El Niño (Figure 2). For both the IOD and Niño3.4 the forecasts suggest conditions similar to the strongest recent IOD or El Niño seasons, i.e. similar to 1994, 1997, 2006 and 2019 for the IOD and 1982, 1997 and 2015 for El Niño seasons. It should be noted, however, that the strong positive 1994 IOD event is quite different from the conditions expected in 2023. The 1994 event was entirely driven by exceptionally cold eastern Indian Ocean SST.

We can gain insights into the relative influence of the WIO and EIO by examining a composite (Figure 3) of standardized SOND SST values for the wettest eastern East Africa SOND rainy seasons (1982, 1997, 2002, 2006, 2011, 2015, 2019). While this figure highlights the expected IOD and El Niño teleconnections, it also emphasizes the very important role played by the western Indian Ocean region. During recent very wet short rains seasons, average observed and forecasts WIO SSTs were both +1.3Z. This is a key forcing region that is very likely to be incredibly warm during the upcoming season (Table 1).

We next examine, together, observed Niño3.4 and IOD values (Figure 4). Note that there is a positive correlation between the IOD and Niño3.4 years. Pooling these seasons (i.e. selecting SOND seasons that are either a strong positive IOD, El Niño, or both, we arrive at six potential analogs. We then exclude 1994 (a strong IOD event) because while it has a similar IOD value, the physical structure of the SST gradient is very different. The WIO in 1994 had average SST values, while the expectation for 2023 is extremely different – and very warm. The logic behind including non-El Niño years (like 2019) is based on the strong, well-documented relationship between exceptionally warm western Indian Ocean SSTs and heavy east African short rains. September 2023 western Indian Ocean SSTs are already very warm, and should the very strong NMME forecast verify, it is likely that these very warm waters will amplify the SOND East African rains. Given the desirability, from a spatial sampling perspective, of having more analogs, using**1982, 1997, 2006, 2015 and 2019**as representative analogs for SOND 2023 seems reasonable. These analogs indicate a high probability of above-normal or very wet rainy season outcomes.

**Results 3. Examining potential Southern Africa Climate Analogs for NDJF 2023-2024**

We next briefly examine potential analogs for southeastern SouthernAfrica – where many studies have identified a link between El Niño and drought tendencies. Figure 5 shows a scatterplot of predicted and observed NDJF SSTs. The color of the circles is based on observed Zimbabwe NDJF rainfall terciles. The selected analog process is straightforward, and based on the expectation of a strong El Niño, similar to 1982, 1991, 1997, 2009 and 2015. This outlook is supported by all of the six NMME models examined (Table 2), as well as current Niño3.4 SST anomalies (~+1.5°C).

**Results 4. Rainfall composite analyses for SOND 2023**

We next present the frequency of above-normal (top tercile) rains, given our set of five analogs (Figure 6). The data used to produce this (and following) figures is available[here](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/laura/Seasonal_analog_maps/NMME.09.2023/). The rainfall response is very widespread, with a 80%-plus likelihood of above-normal rains stretching across Tanzania, Kenya, and much of Ethiopia, Uganda, Eritrea and Somalia. Examining a similar map focused on the chance of a 1-in-5 wet season (Figure 7) suggests that many regions are very likely to experience very wet conditions. Figure 8 shows the median rainfall of the analog seasons, expressed as a percentage of the long-term average. Much of Tanzania, Kenya, Somalia and southern Ethiopia is expected to experience rainfall totals ranging from 150-to-200%. Note that this figure is based on the*median*of the analogs. In specific locations, much more rainfall is likely to occur during the upcoming SOND rainy season, but the exact location of these rainfall extremes cannot be predicted so far in advance. It should also be noted that some of the analog years, like 2015, were extreme in their IOD and El Niño state (Figure 4), but as incredibly wet as seasons like 1997 and 2019.

**Results 5. Somalia observed crop yield outcomes during analog seasons**

We next present observed Somalia Deyr season sorghum crop yields (Figure 9) for selected analog years. Data was not available for all the analogs seasons. Crop yield anomalies indicate a very wide range of possible outcomes. The 1997 Deyr rains were extremely wet, and intense flooding appears related to an almost complete failure of the growing season. In 2006, 2015, and 2019, on the other hand, crop yields were above normal.
