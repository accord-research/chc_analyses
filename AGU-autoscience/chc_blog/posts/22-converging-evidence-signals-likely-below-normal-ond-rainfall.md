# Converging Evidence Signals Likely Below-Normal OND Rainfall

Chris Funk and Laura Harrison

**Recent Observations and Dynamical Model Forecasts**

Concerning signs of poor rainfall performance in the eastern Horn are indicated by the latest weather model forecasts. These are in line with earlier predictions for a likely below-normal October-November-December (OND) 2024 rainfall season. We call attention to the latest rainfall outlooks, which cover the next two weeks to next two months. These collectively warn that southeastern Ethiopia, southern Somalia, and eastern Kenya will have rainfall deficits by late October and that this pattern may continue to impact the region during November and beyond.

Rainfed agriculture is crucially dependent on the amounts and timing of rainfall during October-November in eastern areas– a dry October means extremely limited opportunities in Somalia’s short Deyr season, a dry November in central-eastern Kenya’s lowlands delays cropping activities and production potential, and[hot temperatures](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/products/NMME/Latest/E_Africa/plot_E_Africa_3month_tref_pctl_severe_events_forecast_probability_IC_month.png)during dry spells can quickly draw down moisture and increase the risks of crop failure.

During the next two weeks, from October 10th to the 24th, the GEFS predicts below-average rainfall in eastern Kenya, southeastern Ethiopia, southern Somalia, and northeastern Tanzania. There are very high (> 70%) probabilities of dry conditions during this week. Forecast dry conditions during the two-week outlook period would amount to substantial rainfall deficits in portions of Somalia and southeastern Ethiopia (Figure 1). In these areas, seasonal rains usually peak during October to early November. The CHIRPS-GEFS 15-day forecast anomaly from October 10th also predicts drier-than-average conditions to the east, over the western Indian Ocean. Recent and forecasted atypical winds and very warm sea surface temperatures appear to draw moisture in the central and eastern Indian Ocean, at the expense of the eastern Horn.At this point in a season, the frequently-updated daily GEFS (Figure 1), ECMWF, and weekly SubX models are important in-season forecasting resources. The latest SubX model forecasts, provided by IRI on October 11th, provide converging evidence that the GEFS-forecast dryness will likely continue during late October to early November (Figure 2).

Longer-range seasonal forecasts and outlooks based on these, e.g. from GHACOF, ICPAC, and UCSB CHC, previously highlighted the elevated risks of a below-normal OND 2024 in the eastern Horn. Consistent with these, forecasts from 7 NMME model runs (a group of 124 model runs) made available earlier this week show 40 to 70% chances of below-normal OND rainfall in eastern Kenya, southeastern Ethiopia, and southern Somalia. These concerns have for months been associated with potential La Niña and/or negative Indian Ocean Dipole development, and while these may be of[weak strength](https://iri.columbia.edu/our-expertise/climate/forecasts/enso/current/?enso_tab=enso-quicklook)during October to November, the converging signals from the modeled rainfall, wind, and sea surface temperature forecasts continue to indicate that the developing patterns could be consequential in the eastern Horn.

Since July of 2024, forecasts anticipating very warm eastern Indian Ocean and western Pacific ocean SSTs, combined with the expectation of cool La Niña conditions in the eastern Pacific supported forecasts calling for below-normal OND rains in eastern East Africa. These expectations were founded on east-west sea surface temperature (SST) gradients in the tropical  Indo-Pacific. Dry conditions have historically occurred when the western Indian Ocean has been cooler than the east, and when the western Pacific has been warmer than the eastern equatorial Pacific. Such conditions have now arrived, even though neither a strong negative Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) nor La Niña event developed in September. As we enter the heart of the ‘short’ East African rainy season, conditions in and over the Indian Ocean are particularly important. What we see in reanalysis data is a very strong east-west dipole in atmospheric water vapor, measured as columnar precipitable water vapor (Figure 3-top). This dipole is being supported by low-level winds that are directed more eastward than normal (Figure 3-bottom) from near the eastern Africa coastline to around 80 E along the southern equatorial zone. Near where these anomalous westerly and easterly winds meet in the central and eastern Indian Ocean, SSTs have been very warm, and this appears to be interrupting typical westward moisture flow towards East Africa.

Models are indicating that low-level winds in the eastern Horn and across the Indian Ocean basin could continue to be impacted (Figure 4). The dry conditions of the GEFS week 1 rainfall forecast are associated with predicted disruptions to 850 mb winds in and near Somalia – with more eastward flow than is typical for early October– while a group of WMO ensemble forecast models collectively predict eastward-directed wind anomalies across the equatorial Indian Ocean. This appears to go hand-in-hand with the very warm SSTs predicted in the central and eastern Indian Ocean for OND 2024 (Figure 5).

**NMME OND SST Forecasts Indicate Dry Eastern East Africa Outcomes are Likely**

Since[July](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1418), NMME OND SST forecasts for OND have been associated with patterns indicative of below normal rains. This remains consistent with the latest forecasts from early October (Figure 5). While the western Indian Ocean is likely to have near-normal SST (+0.3℃) the eastern Indian Ocean is expected to be very warm (+0.8℃), leading to a predicted Indian Ocean Dipole value of -0.5℃. The equatorial West Pacific is also expected to be very warm (+0.7℃) while the Niño3.4 forecast is modestly cool (-0.7℃). While the Niño3.4 forecast is a little warmer than the forecast from September, the standardized difference between these two regions – the West Pacific Gradient, remains quite negative (-1.7Z). These forecasts, along with their 80th percentile confidence bounds, are shown in Figure 6. Both the negative WPG and the negative IOD outlook are indicative of below-normal rains.

The 1991-2023 correlation between observed and October NMME WPG values is very high (0.92), and the confidence bounds in the WPG forecast indicate that there is a 90% chance that the OND WPG will be less than -1Z. Since[2016](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=10), the WPG has been a reliable forecast metric, and received further documentation in our 2023[paper](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2023EF003524). Expectations for a very warm Western Pacific (+1.9Z) are a major driver of the negative WPG forecast for OND. A strong WPG may be present, even if Niño3.4 SST anomalies are above the standard -0.5℃ than La Niña event threshold. Since 1982, when the WPG was less than -1Z, six seasons were dry, and 2017 was normal.

The 1991-2023 correlation between observed and October NMME IOD values is also very high (0.95), and the confidence bounds in the WPG forecast indicate that there is a 68% chance that the OND WPG will be less than -0.4℃, a commonly used threshold for negative IOD events. In the past such outcomes were associated with six dry seasons and two normal seasons.

It is very likely, therefore, that the OND season will exhibit a strong negative WPG, a negative IOD gradient, or both.

**Selected OND Analogs**

Analog years for OND 2024 were identified using the IWHG forecast made in October 2024. These same years were also identified last month using the September IWHG forecast. A large number of years (14) represents a relatively wide range of possible IWHG conditions, under the current situation of a yet-to-develop La Niña event and currently neutral IOD conditions. Analogs were identified from years with observed OND IWHG values that were similar to the current forecast value and within 1.28 times the standard error of IWHG predictability, based on analysis of past forecasts and observations (IWHG values between -243 Wm-2+/- 123 Wm-2). This corresponds with the 80th percentile confidence interval. Several years with strong La Niña conditions also fell within that range, but were not selected because a strong La Niña event is unlikely during OND 2024. The analogs based on the September 2024 IWHG forecast are: OND 1983, 1984, 1985, 1995, 1996, 2000, 2001, 2005, 2008, 2016, 2017, 2020, 2021, and 2022.

**A Western V Gradient Forecast for MAM 2024**

We now turn to a forecast of the Western V Gradient (WVG) for March-April-May (MAM) 2025, based standardized NMME ensemble forecasts, which are shown in Figures 9 and 10. The WVG is defined as the standardized difference between Nino3.4 SST and the Western V regions, which are identified in Figure 9. The October R2values for the Western V, Nino3.4 and WVG MAM forecasts are 0.77, 0.65, and 0.74, respectively. The 2025 MAM forecasts for the Western V,  Nino3.4 and WVG are -2Z, -0.5Z, and -1.6Z, with the time-series centered on a 1991-2020 baseline. The WVG is quite predictable and a good predictor of dry East African MAM seasons ([Earth’s Future 2023A](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2023EF003524)).

A scatter  plot showing the 2025 Western V and Nino3.4 forecast along-side historical  1983-2024 values (Figure 10) emphasizes the exceptional predicted ocean state displayed in Figure 9. Following the 2023/24 El Niño, global temperatures reached record-breaking levels that persist into late 2024 ([NASA GISS](https://data.giss.nasa.gov/gistemp/graphs_v4/)). MAM Western V SST, which tracks closely with global average temperatures and the West Pacific Warming Mode ([Earth’s Future 2023B](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2022EF003454)), are predicted by the NMME ensemble to be warmer than any year in the past. At the same time, the models anticipate weak and fading La Niña conditions, consistent with assessments from NOAA. While such conditions are unprecedented in the observational record, past research ([Earth’s Future 2023B](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2022EF003454),[QJRMS 2018](https://rmets.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/qj.3266),[BAMS 2018](https://doi.org/10.1175/BAMS-D-18-0108.1)) has shown that very warm Western V SST can drive stronger Pacific trade winds that amplify the Walker Circulation and increase the probability of dry MAM rains.

An expectation of record-breaking Western V SST and weak negative Nino3.4 SST leads to a forecast of a strong WVG gradient (-1.6Z), with a 90% chance of the WVG being less than -1Z. We suggest two alternate, but similar ways of approaching long-lead forecasts for MAM 2025. Note that neither approach requires that MAM 2025 is ‘in’ a La Niña, though both anticipate La Niña-like conditions.

The first approach uses our documented ([2023A](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2023EF003524)) WVG-based ‘tailored forecasts’. Based on Figure 11, there is more than a 90% chance that the WVG will be less than -0.9Z.  The 1989, 1999, 2000, 2008, 2011, 2016, 2018, 2021 and 2022 seasons exhibited WVG values of less than -0.9Z. In 2018, sub-seasonal MJO events produced a very wet season. Note also that the recent 2023 WVG forecast was a bust – the anticipated strong WVG faded rapidly as the 2023 climate transitioned into an El Nino. All forecasts are uncertain, and conditions could evolve rapidly in early 2025 – but the present WVG outlook anticipates a strong tilt towards dry MAM 2025 conditions. Seven of these nine strong WVG seasons had below-normal rains.

A second approach focuses on MAM conditions following 1998-2022 OND La Niña events ([2023B](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2022EF003454)). These correspond to the 1999, 2000, 2001, 2006, 2008, 2009, 2011, 2012, 2017, 2018, 2021, 2022, 2023 MAM seasons. Eight of these thirteen years had below-normal rains.

La Niña conditions in OND amplify the trade winds, which in a rapidly warming world transport more warm water into the Western V region, amplifying the Indian Ocean branch of the Walker Circulation, producing drier conditions over the Eastern Horn ([2023B](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2022EF003454)). This mechanism explains the increased frequency of back-to-back droughts in OND and MAM, as in 2016/17, 2020/21 and 2021/22. Yet another such a sequence in 2024/25 seems likely, despite the absence of strong La Niña. The lack of strong La Niña or El Nino conditions warrants caution, however. Between October 2022 and February 2023, a very strong WVG dissipated rapidly. This could be happening again, and we may see expectations for MAM WVG conditions fade if a La Niña event fails to develop. The present forecasts for MAM are concerning, however, and an assumption of below-normal rains for eastern East Africa seems warranted.
