# June to September 2026 ‘Extended Early Estimates’ document and anticipate extreme dryness in East Africa, India, and Central America

Authors: Chris Funk, Will Turner, Laura Harrison, Daniella Alaso, Chris Shitote, Johnson Sirengo, Diriba Korecha and Greg Husak

Since their original production in late 2018, the Climate Hazards Center ‘[Early Estimates](https://chc.ucsb.edu/monitoring/early-estimates/info)’ have gained broad use by the agro-climate monitoring community. The Early Estimates area tool for monitoring observed and forecast rainfall conditions. Theseprovide two sets of monitoring products: maps based on observed CHIRPS3 precipitation data, and maps based on CHIRPS3 observations combined with ‘hyper-resolution’ (0.05°) CHIRPS3-GEFS downscaled forecasts. During the middle of the season, these observations+forecasts products can be used to rapidly identify areas with emerging drought conditions. During June and July 2026, El Niño sea surface temperatures have rapidly increased (Figure 1), with Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI) values exceeding +1.6 °C in late July. In East Africa, the CHC’s Early Estimate maps have identified very dry conditions in Ethiopia, Uganda, eastern South Sudan and Western Kenya, with Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) values of less than -2 ([link](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/products/Early_Estimates/v3/Seasonal_Rainfall/with_forecast/africa_east/jun_to_sep/pngs/archive/SPI_13PentAccum_2026_p43.png)), and extremely low rank values ([link](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/products/Early_Estimates/v3/Seasonal_Rainfall/with_forecast/africa_east/jun_to_sep/pngs/archive/Rank_13PentAccum_2026_p43.png)).

![Figure 1. Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI) values as of July 19th, 2026. Graphic downloaded from https://www.bom.gov.au/climate/enso/indices.shtml .](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Figure_rnino_3.4-300x200.png)

Motivated by these concerns, we present here a set of ‘Extended Early Estimates’ (EEE), based on 2026 June-to-July Early Estimates combined with El Niño analog-based projections for August and September. The Early Estimates combine CHIRPS3 observations from June 1 through July 20th (preliminary data for July), along with the July 21st CHIRPS-GEFS forecast for July 21st to July 31st. In addition to the aforementioned dry regions in East Africa, substantial deficits also occurred over parts of India, Indonesia, northern South America and Central America (not shown).

These deficit areas are also regions where, historically, one finds negative rainfall teleconnections during boreal summer El Niño seasons. To capture these influences, we examined all boreal summer El Niños since 1981 (1982, 1987, 1991, 1994, 1997, 2002, 2004, 2015, 2023). The median August–September rainfall totals from these years were calculated, and then added to the June–July Early Estimates to produce global 0.05° Extended Early Estimates of June to September 2026 rainfall totals (geotiff[here](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/will/Requests/Extended_Early_Estimates/extended_early_estimate_jjas_2026_total.tif)). SPI, arithmetic anomalies and percent of mean anomalies were then calculated using a 1996 to 2025 baseline (geotiff of June to September 2026 SPI, anomalies, and percent of mean are available[here](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/will/Requests/Extended_Early_Estimates/extended_early_estimate_jjas_2026_spi.tif),[here](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/will/Requests/Extended_Early_Estimates/extended_early_estimate_jjas_2026_anomaly.tif)and[here](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/will/Requests/Extended_Early_Estimates/extended_early_estimate_jjas_2026_pct_average.tif)). Ranks of the June to September 2026 totals were also estimated, based on a 1981 to 2025 time period (geotiff[here](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/will/Requests/Extended_Early_Estimates/extended_early_estimate_jjas_2026_rank.tif)).

We briefly summarize these results for each of our target regions.

**East Africa – Maps**

EEE results for East Africa (Figure 2) indicate widespread dryness in several countries. SPI results are less than -1.2 or -1.5 across much of Ethiopia, western Kenya, Uganda, South Sudan and southeastern Sudan (left panel). Red pixels in the map of ranks (middle panel), shows places that are expected to be either the driest or 2nd driest season on record (i.e. back to 1981). In Ethiopia, western Kenya and Uganda, some of these regions are (typically) highly productive crop production areas. These areas tend to align with places expected to have rainfall anomalies 200-to-500 mm less than the long term average (right panel). Note that in Ethiopia, there is a large region where rainfall anomalies are expected to be between -500 and -300 mm. These are very large deficits in some of Ethiopia’s most populated and productive crop growing regions. Western Kenya is also a highly productive crop growing region. These areas have been selected for more detailed analysis.

**East Africa – Ethiopia**

The inset in Figure 3 identifies a region of Ethiopia that typically receives bountiful June to September ‘Kiremt’ season rains, but also exhibits a strong link to El Niño. The El Niño teleconnection is described in our June 3rd[blog](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1982), and our[post](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=2013)from July 3rd uses the Seasonal Monitoring and Probability Generator (SMPG) tool to describe why Ethiopia is likely to experience very poor rains. This stacked bar plot shows June-July and August-September rainfall values. The 2026 June–July average of 156 mm is exceptionally low, and on par with very dry years like 1987 and 2015. This value could increase when more station data is used to estimate the ‘final’ July CHIRPS3 in mid-August, but it seems likely that the June–July totals will be very low. The August–September 2026 value shown in Figure 3 (287 mm) is based on the median of the nine El Niño analog seasons. The sum of these two values (443 mm) indicates that extremely low rainfall is expected in June to September 2026. The 2026 June to September Kiremt season in Ethiopia appears likely to be similar to very dry prior seasons like 2015 or 1987, or perhaps even lower. The drought in 2015 had severe impacts in Ethiopia.

**East Africa – Kenya**

After experiencing an exceptionally early start to the season in early March ([blog1](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1923),[blog2](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1968)), western Kenya rains diminished in April and May, and were very low in June and July. A July 22nd field assessment found stunning differences in maize outcomes, based on planting date (left image, Figure 5). Maize planted early reached full maturation, while maize planted later appears to still be at an early vegetative stage. Maize harvest in many maize fields appears to be total failures (right image, Figure 5).

Motivated by these field reports, and guided by the areas with very low ranks in Figure 2, a Kenya focus area was defined (Kenya west and north of 37°E and 0.5°N). Figure 6 shows June–July and August–September totals for this region. As with Ethiopia (Figure 3), the June–July 2026 totals appear likely to be the lowest on record, merely 38 mm over two months. The June–July Early Estimate[anomalies](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/products/Early_Estimates/v3/Seasonal_Rainfall/with_forecast/africa_east/jun_to_sep/pngs/archive/Anomaly_13PentAccum_2026_p43.png)over the humid areas of this region are less than -100 mm, and percent of average[values](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/products/Early_Estimates/v3/Seasonal_Rainfall/with_forecast/africa_east/jun_to_sep/pngs/archive/POA_13PentAccum_2026_p43.png)are less than 30% in many key crop growing regions. The July values are based on CHIRPS3 Prelim though the 20th and CHIRPS-GEFS forecasts for the rest of the months. This means that the July total values might increase modestly once station data is blended into the CHIRPS Final product. But field reports (Figure 5) combined with fairly reliable Early Estimates for June–July, certainly support very dry June–July outcomes in Western Kenya.

The median August–September rainfall for El Niño for western Kenya (70 mm) is used to fill out the 2026 June to September season in Figure 6. This amount is about 70% of the long term average, and indicates a poor chance of recovery for crops that are not near full maturation. The 2026 June to September EEE total is the lowest on record, and outcomes similar to other very dry years like 2002 and 2009 appear likely.

**South Asia – India**

EEE results for South Asia (Figure 7) indicate exceptional dryness in Southern India, and below-normal rains in Thailand.A dry start to the Kharif season in India and the wet-season in Thailand have been of concern for rice growing conditions(GEOGLAM Crop Monitor for AMIS,[June 2026](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/636c12f7f9c2561de642a866/t/6a4664faf0b8224cb5472120/1782998267623/AMIS_CropMonitor_202607.pdf)). As[reported](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c5yzk5rqyr4o)in the BBC, India Meteorological Department (IMD) data found that “India has recorded its driest June in 12 years, and the fifth-driest since nationwide rainfall records began in 1901,” with IMD forecasts indicating below-normal rainfall was expected for July. June 1st to July 22nd national averaged rainfall from[IMD](https://mausam.imd.gov.in/index_en.php)indicated a 19% deficit.

The EEE June–July Early Estimate for southern India (India south of 20°N) was very low, 292 mm, potentially indicating the driest on record back to 1981 (Figure 8). These low rainfall amounts may have inhibited sowing and reduced the area planted ([BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c5yzk5rqyr4o)). A recent India Today[story](https://www.indiatoday.in/science/story/india-monsoon-rainfall-deficit-24-per-cent-2026-2002-drought-el-nino-imd-2949327-2026-07-17)places such a deficit in historic context “*India’s deficit in mid-July 2026 is already bigger than 2002’s full-season shortfall, making this one of the weakest starts to a monsoon in more than two decades. The two figures are not directly comparable, though, because rains through September can still narrow the gap*.”

Unfortunately, conditions reduce the chance of recovery. The El Niño-based estimate for August–September for this region is 358 mm (Figure 8, green bar), which results in a June to September total of 651 mm. Historically, this total would be a bit more than the driest June to September in 2002, but a bit less than the very dry 1987 season. While every drought is different, and changes in social resilience can buffer the impact of climate extremes, large rainfall deficits in[1987](https://theprint.in/past-forward/1987-drought-el-nino-supercomputer/2981999/)and[2002](https://www.unocha.org/publications/report/india/india-drought-ocha-situation-report-no-2)resulted in severe economic and agricultural impacts.

**Central America – Guatemala, Honduras and Nicaragua**

EEE results for Central America (Figure 9) indicate exceptional dryness in parts of Guatemala, Honduras and Nicaragua. Going from left to right in Figure 8, we find substantial areas with SPI less than -1, ranks indicating the 1st, 2nd or 3rd driest season on record, and anomalies less than -100 mm. In Guatemala, Honduras and Nicaragua, FEWS NET[analyses](https://fews.net/latin-america-and-caribbean)identify food crisis conditions in many of these dry areas. April to early August Early Estimate[ranks](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/products/Early_Estimates/v3/Seasonal_Rainfall/with_forecast/america_central_and_caribbean/apr_to_aug/pngs/archive/Rank_25PentAccum_2026_p43.png)also identify many of these regions to be the driest on record. Averaging the June–July early estimates over these three countries suggest a 2026 total of 454 mm, the second driest on record—just a little wetter than 2019.

Unfortunately, the El Niño analog estimate for August–September (532) indicates little relief (Figure 10). Such an outcome would bring the June to September total to 986 mm. This would result in a 226 mm rainfall deficit, when compared to the 1996–2025 mean. Note, however, that 986 mm of rainfall is a lot of water, and the Central American hydro-climatology varies dramatically over small spatial scales. Hence the impact of the observed and anticipated rainfall deficits will vary substantially from location to location, with drier areas more likely to experience greater impacts from water stress.

**Conclusion**

There is a convergence of information, from past El Niño seasons and 2026 observations and short- to long-range forecasts, which underscores concern about negative agricultural impacts in the regions highlighted in this blog. The information presented here integrates several of these information sources, by linking El Niño analogs to the CHC Early Estimates. The Extended Early Estimate is thus grounded by observed rainfall outcomes. It is additionally notable, for the August to September period, that current 30-day to 3-month rainfall forecasts for these regions predict drier-than-normal conditions. The global rainfall forecast from Subseasonal Consortium (SubC) models, from July 23rd is shown[here](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/SubC/30_day/global/pr_mme_latest.png), and the longer-reaching ECMWF and NMME ensemble forecast from early July is available[here](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/products/NMME/Archive/202607/Global/ECMWF-NMME_Global_3month_prec_SPI_moderate_events_forecast_probability_IC_month_202607.png). The implications are that extreme rainfall deficits highlighted by the CHC EEE in East Africa, India, and Central America appear very likely to be realized as the season progresses.
