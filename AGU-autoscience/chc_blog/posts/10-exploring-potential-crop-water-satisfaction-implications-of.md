# Exploring potential crop water satisfaction implications of a very wet, likely two-times normal, 2023 short rainy season in central and eastern Kenya

Chris Funk, Gideon Galu, Frederick Sedah, Will Turner, Greg Husak and Laura Harrison

**Objective**

This post has been prepared to support agricultural decision making in Kenya, in support of the ‘Enhancing Meteorological Networks Partnership’. Using the ‘Water Requirement Satisfaction Index’ (WRSI) modeling framework, we look at onset dates and likely levels of crop water requirement satisfaction. There are other important climate hazards associated with very wet rainy seasons such as flooding, pest and rust outbreaks, and soil inundation that are **not** analyzed in this short post, because they are difficult to model, and Climate Hazards Center lacks the expertise to assess such risks. On the other hand, the CHC has been working with the WRSI for over 20-years, and the WRSI has proven to be a very useful tool for modeling and monitoring agricultural outcomes in Kenya. Here we use the WRSI framework to examine onset date changes and WRSI outcomes for a set of five analog seasons: 1982, 1997, 2006, 2015 and 2019. See this [post](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1315) to see more detailed analysis of the forecasts supporting these analog seasons.

**Identifying likely analogs based on strong El Niño and Indian Ocean Dipole events**

In early September of 2023, all indications suggest that we are very likely to see a very wet 2023 short rainy season. While climate forecasts raised this concern several months ago, due to their expectation of a strong El Niño**and**a strong positive Indian Ocean Dipole event, our confidence in these outlooks has increased dramatically due to the fact that we are now seeing strong positive observed Indian Ocean dipole values (IOD = +1.1C anomaly according to Australia’s Bureau of Meteorology,[BOM](http://www.bom.gov.au/climate/enso/)) and equatorial East Pacific ‘Niño3.4’ SSTs (~+1.6C anomaly according to NOAA’s Climate Prediction Center,[CPC](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/lanina/enso_evolution-status-fcsts-web.ppt)). According to August 29th[BOM](http://www.bom.gov.au/climate/enso/)predictions, these indicators are expected to strengthen by October, reaching +1.5C and +1.9C, based on the average of international climate model forecasts. Recent Climate Outlooks issued by ICPAC’s 65th GHACOF ([here](https://www.icpac.net/documents/755/GHACOF65_Technical_Statement.pdf)) and the Kenya Meteorological Department (KMD) 7th National Climate Outlook Forum ([NCOF](https://meteo.go.ke/sites/default/files/forecast/seasonal-forecast/OND%202023%20SEASONAL%20FORECAST%20OFFICIAL%20STATEMENT.pdf)) call for above-normal rains and early onset dates. The 2023 KMD forecast ([Figure 1](https://meteo.go.ke/sites/default/files/forecast/seasonal-forecast/OND%202023%20SEASONAL%20FORECAST%20OFFICIAL%20STATEMENT.pdf)) calls for a 75% or 85% chance of above-normal rain in central and eastern Kenya. Additional evidence for a wet short rainy season comes from experimental machine-learning-based forecasts produced by NOAA ([here](https://www.psl.noaa.gov/forecasts/s2s_fewsnet/)). These forecasts show a very high chance (>70%) that central-eastern Kenya and Southern Somalia will receive very wet (80th percentile or wetter) seasonal rains in late 2023.

![Figure 1. The KMD 2023 OND rainfall forecast. Obtained from here .](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/KMD_NCOF_Forecast.png)

Here, we will explore likely shifts in growing season onset dates, seasonal and sub-seasonal rainfall totals, and changes in Reference Evapotranspiration (i.e. atmospheric water demand), with a focus on likely agricultural growing conditions in eastern Kenya.  It should be noted that while above-normal short rains will likely provide agricultural opportunities to millions of farmers, extreme rainfall and flooding  likely also pose serious risks.Extreme negative impacts have occurred during past cases when El Niño and positive IOD conditions coincided, such as the flooding disaster that struck Somalia in 1997. River flooding isolated tens of thousands of people, inundated croplands, and necessitated food and medical aid.While extreme rains and flooding are very much a concern for the 2023 short rains, we do not explicitly evaluate such risks in this post.

We begin by identifying a fairly large number of historic analogs, based on August North American Multi-Model Ensemble (NMME) forecasts (Figure 2). This is an update of Climate Hazard Center outlooks made in July (see[here](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1272)for details), and a more detailed September update can be found [here](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1315).

Since July, the general prognosis has remained very stable – with an expectation for a strong positive Indian Ocean Dipole event**and**a strong El Niño event. Based on this expectation, we can identify five similar seasons (1982, 1997, 2006, 2015, and 2019). As described in our September forecast update, 1982, 1997, 2015 and 2006 were associated with both positive IOD and El  Niño conditions. The 2019 was associated with an extremely warm western Indian Ocean – which is a very robust expectation for 2023 as well. The 1994 season, on the other hand, was a very different positive IOD event – a season with neutral western Indian Ocean temperatures, and exceptionally cold eastern Indian Ocean sea surface temperatures. Hence we do not include it as an analog season. While we could select fewer analogs, having a larger sample helps reduce potential random influences from year-to-year variations in the local distribution of storms and/or rainfall deficits. For example, 2019 was a very strong IOD that did not coincide with El Niño, and represents an upper-range of IOD scenarios, associated with challenges in forecasting the timing and peak intensity of IOD.

![Figure 2. Historical SOND Niño3.4 and IOD index values, together with the September NMME Niño3.4/IOD forecast.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Obs_SOND_NINO34_IOD_forecast_plot-1.png)

We begin our analysis with an evaluation of analog rainy season onset dates. These onset dates are based on the standard method used for ‘Water Requirement Satisfaction Index’ (WRSI) modeling. The growing season is assumed to begin when a 10-day ‘dekad’ receives at least 25 mm of rainfall, and the following two dekads receive a total of at least 20 mm. Calculation of the Start Of Season (SOS) is based on 1981-2022 CHIRPS rainfall data. Figure 2 shows mean onset dates for all, mean onset dates for the analogs, and the change between the two. The largest changes are in eastern Kenya where the season typically begins about 10 to 20 days earlier. In the southeast, in Kilifi and Kwale, this corresponds to the onset of the rainy season during September.

![Figure 3. Left and Center – Mean onset dates for all years and the analog seasons. Right – analog season onset dates minus the long term average.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/OnsetDatesAndChanges.png)

While the analogs suggest a modestly earlier start to the season, an analysis of the maximum onset dates for all years and the analog seasons (Figure 4) suggests an even stronger signal when one considers the probability of a timely start in central and eastern Kenya. Deep red areas in the left and central panels correspond to starts in November or December. Such delayed growing seasons are unlikely to be successful. In coastal counties in the southeast (Kwale and Kilifi), the latest onset date in the analogs corresponds to a date in October. Thus, given the analog seasons, assuming a timely start to the rainy season seems well justified.

![Figure 4. Left and Center – Maximum onset dates for all years and the analog seasons. Right – analog season onset dates minus the long term average.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/MaxOnsetDatesAndChanges2.png)

We next explore yield expectations using end-of-season maize WRSI (Figure 5). WRSI measures the ratio of the estimated crop water requirement to the estimated plant available soil moisture. The simulations analyzed here were produced by Will Turner at the CHC. These simulations assume maize crop water requirements. WRSI values of less than 80% indicate mediocre, poor, or failed crop growing seasons. The left panel in Figure 5 suggests that during the eight analogs seasons, such outcomes were infrequent. This does not mean that flooding could not be a problem, but that, on average, crop water requirements were met by the available soil moisture. Comparing these frequencies to the 43 historical record (middle panel, Figure 5) we see a ~40% reduction in the frequency of poor growing seasons. Poor outcomes are much less unlikely given El Niño and/or Indian Ocean Dipole growing conditions. Finally, we examine the change in WRSI in the analogs seasons versus the long-term 1981-2022 average (Figure 5, right). WRSI increases by 20-30% across most of eastern Kenya. While the relationship between WRSI and yields is imprecise, this suggests that substantial increases in yields should be possible in the 2023 short rains, barring issues related excessive moisture, pests and/or plant disease.
