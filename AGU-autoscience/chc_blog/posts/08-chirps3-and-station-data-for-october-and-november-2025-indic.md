# CHIRPS3 and Station Data For October and November 2025 indicate extremely severe drought conditions in Somalia and Central-Eastern Kenya

**Authors**: Chris Funk, Fredrick Sedah, Kidus Belay, Johnson Sirengo, Chris Shitote, Diriba Korecha, Laura Harrison, Daniella Alaso, Robert Saldivar, Pete Peterson, Hannah Button, Will Turner, Shraddhanand Shukla, Mike Budde, Karyn Tabor and Gregory Husak.

**Summary**: This brief post examines CHIRPS3 and station data for October and November, as well as NOAA-PSL Reference Evapotranspiration and CHIRTS-ERA5 temperature data, and concludes that these observations indicate extremely severe drought conditions, as bad as any October–November season on record in Somalia and central-eastern Kenya.

**Introduction**:**Partnerships** **Measure Drought Severity using Integrated Drought Early Warning Systems**

For 40 years, the Famine Early Warning Systems Network (FEWS NET) has led the world in providing timely, accurate, and transparent early warning of food crises, to ensure lifesaving food aid reaches those in greatest need before worst outcomes are realized. “Network” was added to the Famine Early Warning Systems name in 2008 to demonstrate our commitment to building long-term, meaningful partnerships in food-insecure countries. In 2025, in the context of a crushing drought (as detailed below), we can see the tremendous benefits of close collaborations between FEWS NET and the Kenya Meteorological Department (KMD) and the Ethiopian Meteorological Institute (EMI). In Kenya, the University Corporation for Atmospheric Research (UCAR) has worked with KMD and the CHC to fabricate and install 57 3D Printed Automatic Weather Stations (3D PAWS) ([CHC blog](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1840)). 3D PAWS rapidly transmit weather data and provide independent observations from two separate rain gauges—which can really help build confidence when extremely wet or dry rainfall totals are observed. In Ethiopia, since 2020, the EMI has shared a generous set of rainfall observations from first-class stations, and co-developed with the USGS and CHC a Seasonal Monitoring and Probability Generator tool ([SMPG](https://chc.ucsb.edu/tools/smpg)), which serves as the foundation for agro-meteorological reports covering Ethiopia, Kenya and Somalia ([here](https://chc.ucsb.edu/monitoring/east-africa)). For Somalia, very valuable station observations are also provided by the Somalia Water and Land Information Management (SWALIM) and the Somalia Department of Meteorology.

Each month, these stations, and observations from other sources, undergo automated and manual quality control (QC) screenings. The CHC ‘Reality Checks’ team uses a combination of statistical flags and visual inspections to carefully identify and remove stations that appear to be erroneous. The QC-ed stations are then combined with version 3 Climate Hazards Center InfraRed Precipitation estimates to produce CHIRPS3 ‘final’ data (see the[February 2025 CHC blog for more about CHIRPS 3](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1510)). Final data is produced once a month, after the end of the month, and it contains more station observations than the rapidly-updated preliminary CHIRPS product. The CHIRPS3 represents the best-available public rainfall database for the eastern Horn—a public good arising from effective international partnerships.

The CHC, FEWS NET and our partners have developed numerous resources and strategies for monitoring and forecasting drought. Our 2021 book (copyright free material[here](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/chris/DroughtEarlyWarningBook/Final_Full_Book_Drought_Early_Warning_and_Forecasting_subset.pdf)) and two recent presentations provide background on our techniques ([AGNES](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/chris/Talks/2025/AGNES_DroughtEarlyWarning/Funk_Drought_Monitoring_Food_Insecurity_and_Early_WarningSystems.pptx),[FEWS NET](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/chris/Talks/2025/WeatherAndAgBriefing/How_CHC_DefenseInDepth_DEWS_Anticipates_EA_Droughts_v3.pptx)). The goal of these efforts is not to declare drought disasters as this is the purview of national governments, but rather provide a suite of tools that allow for the rapid identification of drought stresses that can impact food security, via negative influences on livelihoods, food access and food availability. Droughts are very often defined in this way—as deficits of water which have impacts on society, nature, or both. Droughts therefore can be seen as the difference between water supply, often provided by rainfall, and water demand—such as the amount of water needed to grow a crop, sustain healthy rangelands, or replenish ephemeral ponds or streams in arid pastoral regions. Drought severity can be a function of the absolute scarcity of water, as measured by precipitation, and the relative scarcity of water when compared to atmospheric water demand. For this, the difference between rainfall totals and reference evapotranspiration (RefET) provides an estimate of the magnitude of moisture deficit when considering the compounding influences of warm, dry, sunny, and/or windy weather conditions. Early warning systems monitor and forecast droughts—they don’t declare them. Describing water deficits is a key aspect of societal resilience, but such descriptions should not be confused with official responses to disasters, such as governmental drought declarations.

**Tracking low November rains using satellite and station observations**

The CHC’s CHIRPS products are unique in that they rapidly combine quality-controlled station data and satellite-based infrared precipitation estimates—which puts the ‘S’ in CHIRP. In recent years, the East Africa CHIRPS products have benefited greatly from new 3D PAWS stations in Kenya and ongoing station contributions provided by EMI and SWALIM. While all of these sources are extremely helpful, the 3D PAWS platforms provide an extra source of corroboration, where each station contains two independent rain gauges. When each gauge records similar extremes, we have added confidence that rainfall truly was exceptional. Unfortunately, as we can see in the Figure 2 ‘Reality Check’ plot below, station observations and CHIRPS3 estimates converged on very low rainfall totals in November. In Somalia, almost every station recorded 0 or near 0 millimeters of rain, in the heart of the Deyr rainy season. In central-eastern Kenya, station values were larger, but still typically far below the long-term average, resulting in the large anomalies shown for November in Figure 3. 3D PAWS stations were a great help in verifying exceptionally low rainfall. For example, coastal Kenya stations in Kilifi recorded just 2.4 and 7.4 mm of rainfall. In Taita Taveta and Embu, rainfall observations were higher (~50 mm), but still 120 or 200 mm below the long-term average. From an agricultural perspective, the large anomalies in southern Somalia, central Kenya, and coastal Kenya all coincide with key crop growing regions.

As of December, NDVI observations showed widespread pasture deterioration across much of the central and eastern rangelands of Kenya and Somalia. A map of December 10th 2025 NDVI, expressed as 2012–2025 ranks, shows that vegetation conditions in most of Somalia appear to be the poorest on record (since 2012). In eastern Kenya, vegetation ranks are higher, ranging from 3 to 5, but still poor. Although the October–November–December (OND) rains would typically provide relief, the season’s early showers were both limited and short-lived. Livestock keepers moved their herds to pockets of vegetation that briefly regenerated at the onset of the season; however, these areas were quickly exhausted due to poor rainfall continuity and the high concentration of animals. As a result, livestock body conditions continue to decline, reducing market value and raising concern over increased mortality.

A comparable situation is unfolding in the cropping zones dependent on rainfall. Many farmers who prepared their fields and planted with the initial rains are now facing widespread crop stress. Crops are wilting and are unlikely to reach maturity under the current moisture deficits. With the remaining time in the OND season insufficient to support full crop development, the region faces an elevated risk of water and food insecurity that may trigger population displacement, hunger and malnutrition.

**Placing the current drought in historic context**

October and November CHIRPS3 Final data can be combined with preliminary CHIRPS observations and 15-day CHIRPS-GEFS rainfall forecasts to produce ‘Early Estimates’ for most of the OND season (Figure 4). The blue and light blue polygons in this figure highlight two areas of special concern: central eastern-Kenya and all of Somalia. Large (more than 100 mm) negative rainfall anomalies cover central-south Somalia and central-eastern Kenya. Time series of October–November rain in Somalia (Figure 5) and central-eastern Kenya (Figure 6) place the drought in historic context. The absolute values of these regional averages are incredibly low—just**33**mm for Somalia and**117**mm for Kenya. These values can be compared with the corresponding 1981–2024 averages of 106 and 219. Somalia and central-eastern Kenya October–November rainfall has been 31% and 53% of the long-term average. Even these totals somewhat obscure how exceptionally dry November was. Somalia and central-eastern rainfall averages for November were just 4 and 57 mm, 9% and 35% of the long-term average. Note that station data (Figures 2 and 3) help confirm these very low levels of precipitation.**Almost no rainfall fell in these regions in November 2025, making this season as bad as any season on record, back to 1981, and similar to previous very hazardous years like 2010.**

To understand the severity of this drought, we can also examine the difference between October–November precipitation totals and atmospheric water demand (RefET). Figure 7 shows the difference between rainfall and RefET (i.e. rainfall minus RefET) for Somalia (y-axis) and Kenya (x-axis). These differences typically range from negative to very negative—water provided by rainfall can be quickly absorbed back into the atmosphere. The Somalia/Kenya values for 2025 were estimated at -338  and -298 mm, respectively.**These data indicate huge gaps between the amount of moisture received and the amount potentially evaporated or transpired. These values are clearly among the lowest on record, and slightly worse than estimates for 2010.**

More sophisticated assessment of crop water satisfaction can be carried out using the Water Requirement Satisfaction Index (WRSI) model. This model uses CHIRPS3 data and NOAA-PSL RefET, along with 20-day downscaled sub-seasonal forecasts from three climate models. Figure 8 shows arithmetic WRSI anomalies for 2025. These represent the predicted end-of-season WRSI minus the long-term average. Only potential crop regions are modeled.**In almost all of Kenya and southern Ethiopia, and much of central-eastern Kenya, the WRSI model results indicate a complete failure—with no effective start to the crop growing season in pink areas. For many of the areas in Kenya and northern Tanzania that do exhibit a start, the WRSI anomalies are very negative—indicative of poor yield outcomes.**Rainfall in the heart of the crop growing season (November) was exceptionally low, and crop production will be compromised. Poor yield could impact food availability and access in remote areas—like Somalia—that depend on importing maize and sorghum.****

Crop and rangeland conditions will also have been degraded by the desiccating effects of warm air temperatures. Conditions between October 21 and November 13th are shown in Figure 9.**Northeast Kenya, southeast Ethiopia and almost all of Somalia experienced above-normal temperatures (left panel), and this occurred in areas that became very very warm, with average maximum temperatures exceeding 35 or even 38°C (95 or 100.4°F).**Some regions in Somalia were the warmest on record.

Low rainfall, high temperatures and high RefET all contribute to poor vegetation conditions. VIIRS ‘Normalized Difference Vegetation Index’ imagery, provided by USGS EROS, can be used to track these influences using satellite observations. Figure 10 shows early December NDVI ranks, for administrative units. This data goes back to 2012.**Dark brown regions arise over much of Somalia, indicating the poorest vegetation conditions in the VIIRS period of record.**In central-eastern Kenya and southeastern Ethiopia, NDVI ranks are also very poor, ranging from the 2nd to 4th lowest values on record.

**Summary**

As anticipated by many groups, the 2025 short rainy season has been poor. But there is a critical difference between poor and very poor. In this post we have marshalled convergent evidence from ground stations, satellites and reanalysis systems to make the case that that very poor rainfall, very high atmospheric water demand, and very high air temperatures conspired to produce severe drought conditions in Somalia and central-eastern Kenya. These severe drought conditions have impacted crop production and rangeland productivity. Historically, these severe water deficits appear as bad as any on record, back to 1981—when reliable satellite observations began.
