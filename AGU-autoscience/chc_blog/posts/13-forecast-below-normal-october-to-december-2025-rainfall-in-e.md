# Forecast Below-Normal October to December 2025 Rainfall in Eastern East Africa

Laura Harrison, Chris Funk, Will Turner, Weston Anderson, Shrad Shukla, Frank Davenport, Daniella Alaso

**Summary**

Forecasts of likely below-average October to December 2025 rainfall in eastern East Africa raise concerns for negative impacts to Deyr and Short Rains season agriculture and rangeland productivity in areas of southern and central Somalia, central and eastern Kenya, southeastern Ethiopia, and northeastern Tanzania. The driving factors are ongoing negative Indian Ocean Dipole conditions and La Niña conditions, which both tend to suppress rainfall in this season and region. Poor short rains could exacerbate the impact of recent[poor harvests](https://www.cropmonitor.org/crop-monitor-for-early-warning-202509#section5)in central-eastern Kenya and Somalia, and poor current rangeland conditions. The drought signal appears most clearly in central and southern Somalia, where analog-based crop modeling suggests a very high probability of delayed or failed onset and poor yields. This blog presents the latest observations and seasonal model forecasts, a near-range outlook for early season rainfall, and agricultural impact scenarios based on historical data.

**The latest information points to below-average early season rainfall**

There are indications that by October 20th, seasonal rainfall deficits of 10 to 50 mm below average could develop in many areas of southern and central Somalia, central and eastern Kenya, northeastern Tanzania, and portions of southern and southeastern Ethiopia (Figure 1-left). Larger deficits are possible in localized areas. In much of coastal Kenya, and most of Somalia and the Somali province of Ethiopia, the expected rains are less than 60 percent of average.

This early-season rainfall outlook comes from the CHC Early Estimates resource, and is based on preliminary CHIRPS3 rainfall estimates for September 1st through October 5th and a 15-day forecast through October 20th (a[CHIRPS-GEFS](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-022-01468-2)forecast bias-corrected to CHIRPS3).

**Agroclimate scenario: Late or unsubstantial rainfall onset in Somalia and low expectations for crops receiving sufficient moisture**

In eastern Kenya and central-southern Somalia, a combination of observations and forecasts indicates a late-or-lacking onset of the crop growing season (Figure 1 upper-right), and a high probability of poor crop water satisfaction (Figure 1 lower-right).

Potential seasonal onset dates and crop water requirement satisfaction index (WRSI) levels are estimated using a combination of preliminary, forecast, and historical scenario-based (analogs) rainfall data. The timing of season onset is based on rainfall accumulations. The crop water level scenario conveys potential seasonal crop water outcomes. These agroclimatic scenarios largely convey regional impacts that could occur if the rest of the season is similar to analogs, and if estimated and forecast rainfall are a good indication of observed conditions through early October.

The September 2025 rainfall amounts are from CHIRPS3 preliminary data, October 1st to 10th rainfall is from the October 1st CHIRPS3-GEFS 10-day forecast, and the majority of the season is completed with dekadal rainfall and reference evapotranspiration data from historical seasons that were selected based on similar forecast conditions and to represent a range of observed outcomes during these years. The WRSI model parameters use water requirements for maize.

In terms of the start of the growing season, it appears likely that most of Somalia may not experience sufficient rains to initiate crop growth, in non-irrigated areas. Crop growing conditions in Somalia during average rainfall conditions are challenging, and an expectation of below-normal rains translates into poor onset conditions and a 60-to-100% chance of below-normal WRSI.

Coastal Kenya presents another area of concern. In this region, onset dates appear likely to be delayed by 3 or 4 dekads, though the overall probability of below-normal WRSI is lower than in Somalia, tending to range from 40 to 75 percent.

Rainfall and satellite vegetation observations should be closely monitored to determine the locations of possible seasonal drought development and the timing of high rainfall periods. Even during seasons with regionally average or below-average rainfall totals, high intensity rain events and/or periodic above-average rainfall can occur.

**Forecasts indicate increased chances of a drier and warmer-than-normal OND 2025**

For several months, international climate model ensemble forecasts have indicated that the OND 2025 season will most likely be drier and warmer than normal. These elevated risks were highlighted by the71st Greater Horn of Africa Climate Outlook Forum (GHACOF 71) and have been incorporated into[food insecurity assessments](https://fsnau.org/downloads/Somalia_IPC_Acute_Food_Insecurity_Malnutrition_Jul2025_Dec2025_Report.pdf)that indicate high levels of food insecurity in Somalia, with almost 2 million children likely to suffer acute malnutrition. The latest forecast updates, discussed here, highlight the need for continued concern.

The latest monthly to seasonal forecasts, based on October initial conditions, are available for the ECMWF SEAS5 and several North American Multi-Model Ensemble (NMME) models. An ensemble forecast using these models (Figure 2, top-left) is similar to earlier pessimistic OND 2025 forecasts, and indicates that in southern Somalia, eastern and southeastern Kenya, and northeastern Tanzania, chances of below-normal seasonal rainfall totals are roughly twice as high as would be climatologically expected. There are 60 to 70 percent chances of below-normal OND 2025 rainfall and greater than 70 percent chances in coastal locations. Models indicate 40 to 60 percent chances in central and northeastern Kenya, southeastern Ethiopia, and across large expanses of Somalia and Tanzania. Above-normal temperatures are forecast across East Africa, with especially high (70 to 90 percent) chances in the eastern and southern zones.

**Ongoing negative Indian Ocean Dipole and La Niña conditions**

These forecasts are consistent with large-scale[climate modes that promote dry](https://chc.ucsb.edu/monitoring/climate-modes)rainy seasons: A combination of negative Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) and La Niña conditions. The OND 2025 forecast update clearly depicts these conditions, showing cool sea surface temperature (SST) anomalies in the western equatorial and central-eastern Pacific Ocean zones and warm SST anomalies across the Indo-Pacific Warm pool region and the Pacific extratropics (Figure 2, bottom).

According to the Australian Bureau of Meteorology, the latest weekly observed IOD index value (to October 5th) was -1.49 °C, which is among the strongest weekly negative IOD values reported in their 2008-to-present[data](https://www.bom.gov.au/clim_data/IDCK000072/iod_1.txt?1759887348911)— similar to values observed during 2010, 2016, and 2022. La Niña conditions emerged in September and will likely persist into early 2026 (NOAA ENSO Diagnostic Discussion[October 9th](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso_advisory/ensodisc.shtml)).

**Tracking model SST and rainfall forecasts**

Seasonal climate model predictions of SSTs in influential zones and of regional rainfall were tracked monthly leading up to the OND 2025 season. The predictability of regional average eastern East Africa (EEA) OND rainfall is relatively high, as models tend to predict rainfall patterns that are consistent with large-scale climate modes (ENSO and Indian Ocean Dipole). Historical September model forecasts of EEA OND rainfall are highly correlated with observed EEA OND rainfall (R1991-2020~ 0.9) (Figure 3).

Generally, model SST and climate mode forecasts tend to support medium-long range seasonal rainfall outlooks, model precipitation forecasts gain skill leading up to the season, and the timing and level of peak skill varies with the target region and season. During 2025 analyses, July forecasts of OND SST in influential ocean regions (see Indo-Warm Pool Heating Gradient,[CHC Blog July 2024](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1418)) yielded moderately higher correlations with observed EEA OND rainfall than the model rainfall forecasts. By September, the model rainfall forecasts were more correlated with observed rainfall. Throughout, the model SST forecasts were useful for identifying years with similar climate conditions.

In regions like eastern East Africa, where predictable seasonal climate conditions have largely consistent influences that reduce or enhance rainfall, analog-based scenarios can provide useful, actionable information. No two rainfall seasons are identical when it comes to seasonal and intraseasonal drivers, spatial and temporal distribution of rains, and the many factors that control crop outcomes and food security conditions. However, a set of historical ‘analog years’ which represents an envelope of anticipated seasonal climate conditions provides a useful means of assessing the range and severity of outcomes in similar years from the past, such as observed rainfall (Figure 3) and crop yields (Figure 4). Analog years were selected through the evaluation of similar past rainfall forecasts and observed and forecast Indian and Pacific Ocean sea surface temperature conditions. The OND 2025 analogs derived from September forecasts are 1995, 1996, 1998, 1999, 2005, 2010, 2016, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2024.

**Concerns for Somalia crop production and regional agricultural and pastoral conditions**

Elevated chances of a drier and hotter-than-average OND 2025 season pose risks for millions of East Africans who depend on rainfed agriculture and pastoralism for food security and livelihood. In Somalia, there is a high risk for rainfed agriculture due to a poor onset of rains and limited opportunities for productive crops. High yields only occur in seasons with abnormally high and consistent rainfall, which appears unlikely this year.

An assessment of historical crop yield data in Somalia identified that in analog years, sorghum yields were below average > 80 percent of the time. The FEWS NET Extended Outlooks project used ten OND 2025 analog years for which data was available to map average yield anomalies and the range of outcomes (Figure 3). Sorghum yields, averaged across Somalia, were below average in eight of the ten analog seasons. There were large yield deficits exceeding 40 percent in five of these seasons (1996-1997, 2005-2006, 2010-2011, 2016-2017, and 2022-2023). Analog season mean yields were 25 percent below average, with a median yield of 35 percent below average. Analysis of maize yields indicated similar outcomes, with an average yield of 22 percent below average.

There are concerns for pastoralist livelihoods due to their strong dependence on access to quality rangeland conditions and water holes. Poor current rangeland conditions are indicated by satellite Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) observations. These show expansive below-average conditions during recent weeks (Figure 5) and during recent months, indicating that natural vegetation dried out earlier than normal after the March to May 2025 rainfall season.Most water holes are at seasonally dry or declining levels in eastern Kenya, southeastern Ethiopia, and Somalia.[](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/NDVI-anomaly-image.png)

If poor pasture growing conditions occur during OND 2025, it could mean a long period before chances of recovery during the next rainy season. After the OND 2025 Short Rains/Deyr season, forecast[hotter-than-normal](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/products/NMME/Archive/202510/E_Africa/ECMWF-NMME_E_Africa_3month_tref_pctl_tercile_events_forecast_probability_IC_month_202510.png)conditions during the hot and dry season will likely quickly erode pasture and water resources.

According to the Famine Early Warning Systems Network (FEWS NET), in Somalia, the anticipated below-average Deyr season, high unemployment, and localized persistent conflict are likely to increase humanitarian food assistance needs. Internally displaced people are among the highly vulnerable groups (FEWS NET Key Message Update[September 2025](https://fews.net/east-africa/somalia/key-message-update/september-2025/print)). In south and southeastern Ethiopia, a below-average season and ongoing pasture shortages would further limit recovery from the 2020-2023 drought. In a worst-case scenario of significantly below-average rainfall, impacts on livestock could drive a rapid deterioration in acute food insecurity (FEWS NET Key Message Update[September 2025](https://fews.net/east-africa/ethiopia/key-message-update/september-2025/print)).
