# From Data to Early Action: Leveraging 3D-PAWS Weather Observations for Drought and other Severe Weather Monitoring and Prediction

![3D-PAWS weather station installed in Kenya. Photo credit: Paul Kucera](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/3d-paws-station-229x300.jpg)

When it comes to saving people’s lives, proactive drought and flood early warnings have proven to be a cost-effective tool for providing evidence-based guidance to humanitarian efforts. Through decades of research and data production, the Climate Hazards Center (CHC) and its international and local partners, have been at the forefront of drought monitoring and prediction across major food insecure regions. This process makes use of satellite-based remote sensing information, meteorological observations, geospatial modeling, and technical expertise from scientists and local experts.

“*Analysis using climate datasets and various tools helps us anticipate significant shocks such as droughts*,” says Dr. Chris Funk, Research Director at the Climate Hazards Center. “*By providing regular and timely updates, we help guide the allocation of finite resources to the most food insecure populations.*”

Climate data gaps can limit the accuracy of drought forecasts and warnings. In many parts of Africa, surface weather observations are sparse and have been declining (Figure 1). In East Africa– one of the most food insecure regions globally– limited resources, political unrest, and civil war has impacted the development and maintenance of weather station networks.

![Figure 1. Left: Map showing the number of stations reporting to CHIRPS v3 across Africa from 1981 to 2025. Right: Time series plot of reporting stations in Kenya over the same period. The number of stations has declined since 1981 but shows an uptick beginning in 2021, attributed to the installation of 3D-PAWS stations.](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/dcalaso/AfricaKenyagif.gif)

To address these challenges, the Climate Hazards Center has partnered with the University Corporation for Atmospheric Research (UCAR), and national meteorological services to deploy 3D-Printed Automatic Weather Stations (3D-PAWS) across several regions. These systems are low-cost and sustainable in that they use reliable microsensors, a micrologger, and locally sourced supplies for the frame and 3D-printers to fabricate sensor housings. So far, there have been more than 55 stations produced and deployed in Kenya, and 16 in Zimbabwe. Each station has two rain gauges, temperature, pressure, and relative humidity sensors that provide observations every 15 minutes. “*3D-PAWS weather stations are revolutionizing how we collect surface weather observations. The mounting frame and electric components of a high-quality 3D-PAWS station can cost about USD $400-500**, be manufactured in about a week, and reprinted if parts fail. This is a game changer for weather monitoring and forecasting,*” says Paul Kucera, the Assistant Director of the COMET program at UCAR.

Some of the 3D-PAWS stations deployed in Kenya are co-located with sites with historically good Global Telecommunication System ([GTS](https://community.wmo.int/en/activity-areas/global-telecommunication-system-gts)) reporting which is useful in data validation. In other words, some of the 3D-PAWS stations are co-located with synoptic stations manned by theKenya Meteorological Department (KMD). Most sites are in data sparse regions to fill in existing gaps in the observational gauge network. For National Meteorological Services, 3D-PAWS stations offer a sustainable solution to expand observation networks and enhance climate services.

“*Data from the 3D-PAWS stations are invaluable in strengthening climate services at the Kenya Meteorological Department*,” says Dr. Richard Muita, Acting Deputy Director climate services at the Kenya Meteorological Department. “*So far, data from these stations have been used in exploratory and data validation studies, and have created new opportunities for real-time monitoring in remote areas where in situ observations were previously limited.*”

In a[study](https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperinformation?paperid=112043)co-authored by Dr. Muita and Dr. Kucera, meteorological observations from a 3D-PAWS station in Kenya were compared with data from a manual synoptic station and a different automatic weather station (Trans-African Hydro-Meteorological Observatory (TAHMO)). The different weather parameters showed high correlation, potentially increasing the role the 3D-PAWS stations can play in remote areas with scarce observation networks.**

Real-time data from the 3D-PAWS stations are freely available on the CHORDS (Cloud-Hosted Real-time Data Services for Geosciences) project servers:[https://3d-fewsnet.icdp.ucar.edu/](https://3d-fewsnet.icdp.ucar.edu/)(Kenya), and[https://3d-zimbabwe.icdp.ucar.edu/](https://3d-zimbabwe.icdp.ucar.edu/)(Zimbabwe).

**3D-PAWS weather observations in action**

The[East Africa Agroclimate Special Report](https://chc.ucsb.edu/monitoring/east-africa)demonstrates the application of 3D-PAWS data in operational climate monitoring. The report provides seasonal analysis up to the current dekad (10-day periods), and outlooks to the end of the season for Ethiopia, Somalia, and Kenya. Each dekad, [CHIRPS](https://chc.ucsb.edu/data/chirps3) data is blended with additional station observations from the Ethiopian Meteorological Institute, Somalia Department of Meteorology, FAO SWALIM, and 3D-PAWS rainfall data from the Kenya Meteorology Department to provide Improved Rainfall Estimates (IRE).

The Seasonal Monitoring and Probability Generator ([SMPG](https://www.chc.ucsb.edu/tools/smpg)) tool is then employed to provide analyses of rainfall conditions. The SMPG is a new, user-friendly QGIS-based tool that helps users monitor the progress of the rainy season. The SMPG tool does this by comparing the IRE data with long term averages and historical records from select (analog) years. Analog years are those that are historically deemed to depict similar synoptic or global weather patterns with the current period being considered. The SMPG tool also generates an outlook that provides a range of scenarios for the End of Season concatenating season-to-date totals,[CHIRPS-GEFS](https://chc.ucsb.edu/data/chirps-gefs)forecast data for up to 15 days, and historical data from user-selected analog years. For example, a user cancalculate the percentile ranking of the estimated seasonal rainfall accumulation from the Start of Season to the current dekad (Figure 2-left) and the probability of above- or below-normal rains at the End of the Season (Figure 2-right).

![Figure 2. Left: The map depicts the percentile ranking of the seasonal accumulation of rainfall for the period March 1st to April 30th, 2025. The names displayed on the map are district names in Kenya, Somalia and Ethiopia. Right: The map displays the percent probability of below-normal rains (defined as the driest one-third of all events) at the end of the season using observed data March 1st to April 30th 2025, and scenarios based on selected analog years. The latest report and more information on the analog years can be found at https://chc.ucsb.edu/monitoring/east-africa .](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/ethiopia-3d-paws-blog-1-1024x544.jpg)

Through regular sub-national assessments of rainfall and vegetation conditions, CHC and other key partners are able to identify the location, extent, and severity of mid-season drought.

“*Using these dense networks of 3D-PAWS stations in Kenya, we are able to really zoom into the county  levels providing climate information to local decision makers and international agencies,*” explained Diego Pedreros, Researcher at CHC.

The application of 3D-PAWS observations goes beyond drought identification and forecasting. The data can also be leveraged in flash flood warnings and improving numerical weather prediction, among other applications.
