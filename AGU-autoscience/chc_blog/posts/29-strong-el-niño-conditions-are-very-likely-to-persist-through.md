# Strong El Niño conditions are very likely to persist through late 2023

By Chris Funk and Laura Harrison

**Summary**

Since the summer of 2023, monitoring and forecasting groups have been warning that the ongoing El Niño event would most likely intensify during late 2023 to early 2024, to join a relatively short list of historically strong events. Recently, observed weekly sea surface temperature (SST) anomalies in the Niño 3.4 region were very warm – greater than +1.5 °C.

Here, we take a look at how September 2023 SSTs compared to past strong El Niños and use that historical data to identify what changes in intensity might be anticipated. September Niño3.4 monthly SST values have historically been very good predictors of next 3-month Niño3.4 SST.

Consistent with previous model forecasts, this analysis identifies that the historically strong Niño3.4 SST anomalies in September are likely to be a precursor for continued warm Niño3.4 conditions. Observed September 2023 SSTs and historical trends support outlooks for strong El Niño conditions through 2023. A regression-based prediction using September 2023 conditions identifies that a ~1.8 °C with Niño3.4 SST anomaly is likely during October-November-December (OND) 2023; in other words, warmer than most events, but likely not reaching the historical extreme values observed in 1982, 1997, and 2015. These empirical results are consistent with the early October CPC/IRI[outlooks](https://iri.columbia.edu/our-expertise/climate/forecasts/enso/current/).

**Measuring El Niño strength**

El Niño is a coupled ocean-atmosphere phenomenon. Its characteristics are[closely monitored](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/enso-detection-and-monitoring-depends-data)using a variety of indicators – surface and subsurface ocean characteristics and air pressure and winds at multiple heights – to ascertain if telltale signs of El Niño are present and how these may evolve. One of the key indicators used to measure event strength is the degree by which sea surface temperatures rise above typically cooler levels in the equatorial central-eastern tropical Pacific Ocean (the Niño 3.4 region).[NOAA’s probabilistic strength forecasts](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso_advisory/strengths/index.php), for example, define strong events as those having 3-month Niño 3.4 SST anomalies that are greater than +1.5 °C.

**How warm was the central-eastern Pacific in September 2023?**

At present time, the average Niño3.4 SST for September offers a timely measure of recent event evolution. Other oceanic and atmospheric variables are consistent with El Niño, and continued development is indicated by model forecasts from climate monitoring agencies. See, for example,[NOAA Climate Prediction Center](https://iri.columbia.edu/our-expertise/climate/forecasts/enso/current/)and[Australia Bureau of Meteorology](http://www.bom.gov.au/climate/enso/)ENSO updates. These forecasts indicate very warm OND Niño3.4 SST conditions.

Examining the latest September Niño3.4 SST anomalies (Figure 1) from the NOAA version 5 Extended Reconstruction SST dataset, and applying a 1.5 °C threshold for a strong event to monthly average values, the data indicates that September 2023 Niño3.4 SST was +1.7 °C higher than the 1991-2020 average, exceeding that +1.5 °C threshold.

As shown as a time-series plot (Figure 1), the September 2023 warmth was exceptional given the historic record, though not the warmest on record. Going back to 1920, when we begin to have reliable ship track data in the equatorial eastern Pacific, we find that the 2023 value was warmer than 96% of previous Septembers (119 out of 123). Three Septembers were warmer- in 1982, 1997 and 2015- while 1972 had a similar anomaly.

**Predictions for October-November-December 2023 Niño3.4 SST, based on September 2023 conditions**

El Niño events tend to form in the latter half of a calendar year, reach maximum intensity near the end of that year or the beginning of the next, and then weaken during the first part of that year. Historically, September Niño3.4 conditions have been highly accurate predictors of average October-November-December (OND) Niño3.4 SST anomalies.

We show this here using a 1982-2022 baseline, to align with the period in which climate model observations are available, and a simple regression analysis between historical September and OND Niño 3.4 anomalies. The September SST anomalies are extremely well correlated with OND SST anomalies, with a 1982-2022 correlation of +0.99. A linear regression  slope value of slightly greater than 1 (1.07), leads to an expectation that OND 2023 Niño 3.4 SST anomalies might increase to +1.8C, with 80% confidence intervals of +/- 0.2 °C (Figure 2). Hence, the persistence exhibited in the past suggests a > 90% chance of strong OND 2023 El Niño conditions (based on Niño3.4 SST), given observed SST conditions in September. This is similar to the 77% chance given by[NOAA’s probabilistic strength forecasts](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso_advisory/strengths/index.php).

**Relating September Niño34 SST times series to OND ERA5 reanalysis Central Pacific atmospheric heating**

Given the non-stationarity of SST patterns, due to global warming, it can be somewhat unclear what a specific Niño3.4 SST anomaly means, in terms of its ability to disrupt atmospheric circulation patterns. To help quantify such disruptive influences, we can use measures of atmospheric heating, derived from atmospheric reanalyses such as the ERA5, measured in Watts per meter squared (Wm-2). Atmospheric heating over the equatorial Central Pacific (160°E-140°W, 8°S-6°N) is linked to enhanced precipitation in this region, and at long enough timescales, can indicate the intensity of El Niño climate disruptions. Atmospheric heating arises through the latent heat released by precipitation, the convergence of heat by low-level winds, and through the trapping of longwave radiation by water vapor. This heating drives El Niño teleconnections.

This indicator of atmospheric forcing intensity, using September 2023 data and an outlook for OND 2023, suggests that a strong atmospheric response can be anticipated through the rest of 2023. That OND 2023 estimate places the forcing intensity as being one of the top six events since 1982, and likely with lower intensity than the extreme 1982, 1997, and 2015 values. Figure 3 shows a scatterplot with observed September Niño3.4 anomalies on the x-axis and ERA5 East Pacific heating on the y-axis. The heating values are expressed in absolute terms to emphasize the influence on the atmosphere. In non-El Niño seasons, heating over the East Pacific is low (< 200 Wm-2), and sometimes even negative. When Niño3.4 SST anomalies range from about +0.5 to +1 °C we see little change in forcing, with values of about +200 Wm-2.  The strongest El Niños, on the other hand, were associated with heating anomalies of about 700 Wm-2. Since 700/200 = 3.5, we can infer that the forcing of strong El Niños is much greater than the forcing associated with weaker events. A ~+1.7 °C event is likely to be much more energetic than a +0.7 °C event. By using the historical relationship between observed September Niño 3.4 anomalies and observed ERA5 OND East Pacific heating, we can arrive at an approximation of the OND East Pacific heating rate of about +540 Wm-2. This is less than the very strong 1982, 1997 and 2015 El Niño events, and similar to the 2002 OND value of 577 Wm-2. Based on this indicator of atmospheric forcing, this El Niño appears strong.

**Examining September Niño3.4 SST North American Multi-Model Ensemble forecasts**

North American Multi-Model Ensemble (NMME) forecasts also indicate strong El Niño conditions for OND (Figure 4). As in previous posts, we use the latest NMME ensemble to predict OND Niño3.4 SSTs, and arrive at exactly the same forecast: +1.8°C, although with a slightly higher standard error.

Hence lagged Niño3.4 relationships (Figure 2), lagged Niño3.4-Atmospheric Heating relationships (Figure 3), and the latest NMME forecasts all converge on support for the assumption of strong OND 2023 El Niño conditions.
