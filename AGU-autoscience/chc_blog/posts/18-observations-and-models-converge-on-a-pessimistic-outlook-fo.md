# Observations and models converge on a pessimistic outlook for the upcoming eastern East Africa long rains

Chris Funk and Laura Harrison

**Stages of Prediction: How the latest observations support elevated risks of drought in Eastern East Africa**

The best forecast systems take a staged approach to early warning, beginning with long lead climate forecasts, and then incorporating observations, weather forecasts and sub-seasonal outlooks to heighten our certainly regarding outcomes. In some cases, a clear understanding of the common drivers of extremes can enhance our certainty. When observed climate anomalies align with known drivers of droughts or floods, we can gain an enhanced ability to act. Such was the case, for example, in March 2021 and September 2023, when CHC posts ([2021](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=946),[2023](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1315)) discussed how eastern East Africa was very likely to experience either dry conditions or extreme precipitation.

Today, on February 17th, we examine the latest conditions related to the upcoming March-April-May (MAM) eastern East African rainy season. We begin with conditions, then proceed to look at CHIRPS-GEFS weather forecasts, sub-seasonal forecasts from the IRI, seasonal climate model forecasts, and Western V Gradient outlook maps based on the analogs described in our earlier February posting ([here](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1516)).

Figure 1 shows vegetation anomalies over eastern Africa. Approaching the MAM rainy season, much of the eastern East Africa (outlined in red) region shows below-normal vegetation conditions, indicating moisture stress in cropping areas and rangelands.

As discussed in our previous[post](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1516), we are concerned about sea surface temperature (SST) forecasts anticipating a strong ‘Western V Gradient’, which can elevate the chances of below-normal rainfall in eastern East Africa. Figure 2 shows a WVG-based precipitation forecast, based on the median of 9 previous strong WVG seasons. Together, Figures 1 and 2 raise concerns about poor MAM rains in many areas that are already facing abnormally arid conditions.

Strong WVG conditions are associated with more precipitation near Indonesia and northern Australia, and disruptions in low-level wind fields over the Indian Ocean. Such conditions can reduce the flow of moisture into eastern East Africa, and bring dry subsiding air down over the Horn, reducing the chance of bountiful rains. In the first part of February, reanalysis data from[NOAA](https://psl.noaa.gov/)exhibit exceptionally strong rainfall rates (Figure 3) and low-level wind anomalies (Figure 4). Together, these figures describe an ‘Indian Ocean Dipole’-like response, with extremely heavy rainfall in the equatorial eastern Indian Ocean (Figure 3) associated with exceptionally strong (~10 ms-1) eastward wind anomalies over the southeastern equatorial Indian Ocean. This ‘IOD-like’ response, however, is primarily being driven by exceptionally warm ocean conditions near Australia and Indonesia.

While these observed anomaly patterns blend weather and climate signals, and will shift over the coming months, they help amplify our concerns, because they resemble responses seen in previous dry MAM seasons.

**Sub-seasonal and seasonal climate model forecasts provide a convergent and concerning outlook for the eastern Horn of Africa region**

As we approach the MAM season, we are in a good position to look at increasingly skillful outputs from climate models. Sub-seasonal March 1-14 rainfall forecasts from IRI (Figure 5) indicate elevated chances of poor rains at the outset of the rainy season. Looking out over the entire MAM season, the recently-updated World Meteorological Organization (WMO) multi-model ensemble forecasts of rainfall and low-level winds (Figure 6) describe conditions typically associated with a strong Western V Gradient season. Rainfall near Indonesia is above normal, while rainfall is suppressed over the equatorial western Indian Ocean, eastern East Africa and near the equatorial dateline (~180 °N/S). The bottom panel of Figure 6 displays the associated wind anomalies, which broadly follow recent observations (Figure 4).

The WMO, North American Multi-Model Ensemble and CS3 dynamical climate model forecasts all provide a convergent expectation for below-normal MAM rains (Figure 6).

**Conclusion – convergent evidence for concern, but uncertainty remains**

The CHC has anticipated strong Western V Gradient conditions, and elevated chances of a poor MAM season for many months, but in mid-February we have a lot more sources of information. Recent observed climate patterns and dynamic model forecasts paint a convergent and concerning picture. The confidence surrounding a pessimistic outlook has been increased by the convergence of all these sources of information. Nevertheless, uncertainty remains, because sub-seasonal weather patterns can bring extreme rains during any season. Nevertheless, the evidence presented here certainly supports preparations for poor rains in eastern East Africa.

Madden Julian Oscillation (MJO) events can also influence seasonal rains, such as the very wet conditions during MAM 2018. A retrospective study of that season ([Kilavi et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/atmos9120472)) also highlighted the influence of tropical cyclones. As of February 13th, MJO phase forecasts for late February to early March show a large range of possibilities, but generally do not point to strong MJO activity that would greatly enhance rains in equatorial East Africa during that time. Close monitoring of observed rainfall, MJO conditions, and forecasts from short-range weather and sub seasonal climate models, will be important for this Long Rains season.
