# Field Assessments Strengthen FEWS NET Early Warning Capabilities

Daniella Alaso, Greg Husak, Laura Harrison

Field assessments play a critical role in the early warning of acute food insecurity by the Famine Early Warning Systems Network ([FEWS NET](https://fews.net/)). Regional scientists stationed in Africa, Asia, and Central America are integral in the ability of FEWS NET to understand and report on the significance of agroclimatic conditions by integrating remotely sensed estimates with field observations. For more than two decades, regional scientists have embarked on crop tours to help verify key information such as the start of the rainy season, planting dates, access to seed and fertilizer, extreme weather events, rangeland quality, the state of soil and surface water resources, and crop phenology and yields.

**2025–2026 Drought in East Africa**

Recent field assessments in East Africa confirmed the devastating impacts of a poor 2025 October–December rainy season on lives and livelihoods. During a crop tour held in January 2026 in Kenya, field teams observed widespread crop losses in many of the assessed regions, with little to no prospects for harvest.

“*I have been farming for a long time now. Each year is different from the previous. But this year seems to be the worst I have ever experienced. The seeds never left the ground (germinated),*” one Kenyan farmer said.

“*Almost all the watering holes were dry, forcing pastoralists with their livestock to walk longer distances in search of water*,” noted Chris Shitote, the Regional Scientist for East Africa.

The severity of the situation in the eastern Horn was reflected in[CHIRPS](https://www.chc.ucsb.edu/data/chirps3)rainfall estimates, which indicated large deficits (more than 100 mm) in key crop growing areas of Kenya at the peak of the short rains/Deyr 2025 season (CHC[Blog 2](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1905)). By the end of the rainy season in December, the crop water requirement satisfaction index (WRSI) confirmed that the rainfall and crop growing season had failed to establish in most areas of Kenya. Shitote’s field validations in January 2026 showed that the dry conditions led to the failure of crops as they entered early stages of development (Figure 1).

During the boreal spring and summer of 2025, FEWS NET scientists had tracked outlooks for the potential development of La Niña and negative Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) conditions, which typically result in a below-average short rains/Deyr rainfall season in eastern East Africa. Model forecasts of precipitation and sea surface temperatures in the equatorial Pacific and Indian Oceans were used to develop most-likely scenarios that FEWS NET regional scientists used to communicate the risks of below-average seasonal rains. These risks were also communicated at the 72nd Greater Horn of Africa Climate Outlook Forum ([GHACOF 72](https://www.icpac.net/events/ghacof-72-advancing-climate-services-for-resilient-communities-and-sustainable-development-in-the-greater-horn-of-africa/)) attended by FEWS NET scientists – Chris Shitote and Dr. Chris Funk.

Observations, seasonal and sub-seasonal forecasts all converged on a very poor start of the season (CHC[Blog 1](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1873), CHC[Blog 2](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1873)). A negative Indian Ocean Dipole had developed during August and La Niña conditions had emerged in September. Associated strong sea surface temperature gradients then reinforced weather conditions that suppressed rainfall and produced hot, dry conditions in the eastern Horn. Rangeland conditions had been previously impacted by poor spring season rains, in some of the affected areas, and a hotter-than-normal June–September dry season rapidly dessicated rangeland (FEWS NET.[East Africa Seasonal Monitor September 4, 2025](https://fews.net/east-africa/seasonal-monitor/september-2025)). The poor performance of rainfall in late 2025 made these challenges more severe and long-lasting. In November, the government of Somalia declared a National Emergency. Soon thereafter in December, FEWS NET[warned](https://fews.net/eastern-horn-africa-faces-severe-food-security-risks-after-record-low-rainfall)that more than 3.5 million people in Kenya and 5 million people in Somalia would be in need of humanitarian food assistance through May 2026.

**Conditions in Southern Africa**

Seasonal conditions in Southern Africa followed a different trajectory. At the same time that October–December rains were failing across parts of East Africa, adequate rains in many areas of southern Africa supported healthy crop establishment and the October–May 2025/26 season was off to a good start across most areas. Favorable soil moisture conditions, reflected in above-average WRSI values, supported healthy plant germination. Field observations and interviews with agricultural officers in Zimbabwe and Mozambique in January 2026 confirmed that the season was well-established, with many areas seeing a timely or early onset. Above-average rainfall in November and December helped restore pasture conditions, resulting in good livestock health and improved cattle prices. Agricultural officials noted that these improvements were especially important following a poor 2024/25 season which saw grain stocks depleted after only 3–4 months, compared with the typical 9–10 months.

In some areas of the region the seasonal rains were doing too well, October 2025 to January 2026 CHIRPS rainfall totals for parts of southern Mozambique and Zimbabwe were the highest on record, since at least 1981. Flooding concerns emerged in low-lying and riverine areas. Several weeks of heavy rains left soils waterlogged, and rising rivers began overflowing their banks and flooding farms.

“*In Gaza, Mozambique low-lying maize fields were flooded, and farmers were concerned that continued flooding could significantly reduce harvests,*” noted Pamella Mogane, Assistant Regional Scientist for Southern Africa. “*Discussions with agricultural officers indicated that the government had begun encouraging farmers to harvest green maize to avoid total crop losses due to water damage given the expectations of continued rainfall.*”

**Capturing Insights that Satellites Alone Cannot Provide**

FEWS NET scientists continue to emphasize the importance of field assessments, noting that on-the-ground observations provide a unique perspective on challenges faced by farmers and their adaptive strategies.

“*In Guatemala, for instance, many farmers are transitioning from maize to coffee and vegetable production due to increasingly erratic rainfall patterns and the high water demands of maize,*” said Mario Rodriguez, the Regional Scientist for Central America. These shifts have important implications for agricultural monitoring and highlight the need to continuously update crop calendars and model assumptions.

“*In Zimbabwe our recent field assessments informed us that some farmers delayed planting after previous experiences of false starts to the rainy season,*” explained Tamuka Magadzire, the Regional Scientist for Southern Africa. Such information is essential when interpreting seasonal crop monitoring products. If crop water models such as the WRSI assume a longer growing cycle than the farmers use, for example, they may misrepresent crop phenology, climatic sensitivities, and overall seasonal crop performance.

**Collaboration as a key to success**

FEWS NET regional scientists have also been instrumental in strengthening partnerships with farmers, local institutions, and government agencies. Sustained engagement and partnerships builds trust, facilitates knowledge exchange, and helps ensure that scientific tools and data products respond to local needs. One example is the Mesas Agroclimáticas (Agroclimatic Roundtables) in Guatemala—collaborative forums where scientists, agricultural extension officers, and farmers jointly discuss seasonal forecasts, climate risks, and agricultural planning. Because the Mesas Agroclimaticás are held three times a year, they create a valuable feedback loop. “*Farmers provide feedback on forecast accuracy, which helps refine future forecasts,*” explains Mario Rodriguez. “*They are also empowered to seek advice on when to begin planting, which seeds to use and the timing of fertilizer application*.*During the growing season, farmers share information on how conditions are progressing and how forecasts may impact the remainder of the season*.”

![Figure 4. Farmers receiving rainfall forecasts and information on crop production patterns and expected impacts on crop growing conditions (top left). Farmers discussing activities to mitigate potential negative impacts during the forecast period (top right). Community leaders holding the final report from the Mesas Agroclimaticás, which will be shared with other farmers to provide recommendations for improved agricultural activities in their villages (bottom)](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Guatemala_MesasAgroclimaticas-Mar2026-300x198.jpg)

Through these engagements, FEWS NET scientists can translate complex climate and remote sensing information into locally relevant insights while also learning directly from farmers and local experts. This two-way exchange strengthens early warning systems and ensures that climate information better supports agricultural and humanitarian decisions that protect lives and livelihoods.

**Summary**

Regional scientists play a crucial role in supporting food insecurity early warning efforts by FEWS NET.  In addition to their cutting edge analysis, their regular presence and interactions builds trust across institutions and communities. These relationships with partners allows them to both access information collected by partners and amplify messaging through partner networks, increasing the reach and impact of FEWS NET work. The many facets of the field scientists’ role help build credibility and add visibility to FEWS NET reporting.
