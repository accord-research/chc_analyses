# A rapid transition to strong El Niño and positive IOD conditions threatens Eastern and Southeastern Africa with Climate Extremes

**Authors**: Chris Funk,Laura Harrison,Gideon Galu,Diriba Korecha, Tamuka Magadzire, Nachiketa Acharya,Andrew Hoell – NOAA Federal, Weston Anderson, Diego Pedreros,  Chris Shitote, Pam Mogane, Will Turner,Shraddhanand Shuklaand Greg Husak

**Section 1. Focus**

This post will focus on forecasts of Niño3.4 Sea Surface Temperatures (SST) – a measure of El Niño strength during the July-August-September (JAS), September-October-November-December (SOND), and November-December-January-February (NDJF) seasons. We will also examine Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) conditions during SOND, when IOD teleconnections are typically strongest.

While El Niños and IOD events affect many regions of the world, here we will focus on likely impacts in three regions and seasons.

1. **Northern Ethiopia/JAS**– a large area of north-central Ethiopia stretching from 7°N-14°N and 36.5°-40.5°E. In 2015, this El Niño-impacted region (BAMS climate change attribution[study](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/bams/97/12/bams-d-16-0167.1.xml)),  experienced its worst March-to-September drought in 50 years (FEWS NET[report](https://fews.net/east-africa/ethiopia/special-report/december-17-2015)). In 2002 (FEWS NET[report](https://fews.net/sites/default/files/documents/reports/Ethiopia_200409en.pdf)) and 2009 (FEWS NET[report](https://fews.net/sites/default/files/documents/reports/Ethiopia_200409en.pdf)) El Niño-related droughts also disrupted crop production, leading to increases in food insecurity. Now, another El Niño may bring dry conditions in critical crop growing regions of a very food insecure country, though antecedent soil moisture conditions are above normal because March-April-May (MAM) 2023 was exceptionally wet. JAS composites also include South Sudan and southern Sudan.
2. **Eastern East Africa/SOND**– Ethiopia, Somalia and Kenya east and south of 38°E and 8°N. This region is heavily influenced by the SOND ‘short’ rainy season, which varies dramatically from year-to-year. In 2019, strong positive IOD conditions contributed to exceptional rains, flooding, displacement and a widespread desert locust outbreak (FEWS NET[report](https://fews.net/east-africa/special-report/january-29-2020)). In 2020, 2021, and 2022, climate change-enhanced La Niña conditions contributed to a sequence of five dry seasons in a row (multi-agency[alert](https://fews.net/sites/default/files/Joint%20Alert%20on%202023%20MAM%20Rains%20Final_0.pdf)). Now, in late 2023, the effects of El Niño will likely combine with the influence of a very strong IOD event to bring a very rainy season, which will follow an exceptionally wet MAM season in Ethiopia and Kenya due to the recurrent of Madden-Julian Oscillation and emerging of a strong tropical cycle in the Indian Ocean.
3. **Zimbabwe/NDJF –**Using national-average rainfall and by-pixel rainfall composites, we suggest that a strong NDJF El Niño event appears likely, and that**if**a strong El Niño arises, it is likely that much of Zimbabwe will receive poor rains. In 2002/03 and 2015/16, El Niño-related droughts led to reductions in crop production and increased food insecurity (FEWS NET reports[2003](https://fews.net/sites/default/files/documents/reports/Zimbabwe_200301en.pdf),[2016](https://fews.net/southern-africa/zimbabwe/food-security-outlook/february-2016)).

Please note that we have prepared an accompanying post ‘**Predictable Extremes Can Inform Proactive Interventions’**([here](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1287)) that discusses how climate change is interacting with natural El Niño, La Niña and IOD variations to produce more frequent opportunities for forecasts and proactive risk management.

**Section 2. Forecasts of Niño3.4 and IOD SSTs**

The SST forecasts presented here are drawn from the July update of the North American Multi-Model Ensemble (NMME) climate forecasts. Forecasts for six modelsare extracted over the Niño3.4 region and the western (10°S-10°N, 50°E-70°E, WIO) and eastern (10°S-0, 90-110°E, EIO) IOD index areas. A multi-model weighted average is then constructed, based on how well each model recreates the variance of the observed 1982-2022 Niño3.4, WIO and EIO time-series. A bivariate regression is used to produce our final forecast. The standard errors of these forecasts are used to derive 80thpercentile confidence intervals.

Figure 1 shows scatterplots depicting predicted and observed NMME forecasts for standardized Niño3.4 SSTs in JAS, SOND and NDJF. Strong skill is identified in all seasons, with R2values of greater than 0.78. The forecasts for all seasons suggest a strong El Niño, with forecast Niño3.4 values of about +1.5Z ± 0.5Z. This suggests SST values that will most likely be similar to 1982, 2002 and 2009, but very warm conditions such as in 1997 and 2015 are possible. The regression-based confidence intervals suggest that there is about a 90% chance Niño3.4 SSTs will exceed about +1Z. These ‘strong El Niños’ are associated with large disruptions in the Walker Circulation (See Figure 3 in thispost). The regression estimates also suggest that there is about a 10% chance that Niño3.4 SSTs will be less than about -1Z.

Please note that the observed standard deviation of the Niño3.4 SST time-series is about one degree Celcius so +1Z and +1C are almost identical. Figure 3 in our companion post ([here](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1287)) relates our Niño3.4 forecasts to absolute values of Niño3.4 SSTs and the strength of the observed atmopsheric heating gradient changes over the equatorial Pacific. Strong El Niños, with standardized or non-standardized anomalies of great than +1Z/+1C, have Niño3.4 SSTs of more than 27.7C. In the past, these have been associated with large changes in circulation over the equatorial Pacific Ocean. These changes can drive changes in teleconnected regions.

The color of the circles in the left, middle and right panels of Figure 1 correspond to wet, normal or dry rainfall outcomes in, respectively, northern Ethiopia, Eastern East Africa, and Zimbabwe. Wet and dry seasons fall into the top and bottom thirds of the 42-year CHIRPS record. While not every season follows the general pattern, we see a strong propensity for dry seasons in Ethiopia and Zimbabwe, and wet seasons in Eastern East Africa.

**Section 3. Forecasts of IOD SSTs**

Figure 2 shows SOND forecasts for the West and East Indian Ocean. Also shown is a standardized IOD index, based on the West-minus-East gradient. Only SOND results are shown, since empirical comparisons with Ethiopian and Zimbabwe rainfall did not identify strong IOD teleconnections. One striking component of these forecasts is the prediction of very warm west Indian Ocean temperatures, temperatures on par with extreme seasons like 2015 and 2019. The R2value for this region is high (0.70). Five models are predicting that this will be the second warmest west Indian Ocean on record. One model (NASA-GEOSS2S) anticipates the warmest on record. Forecasts for the east Indian Ocean are less extreme, but still quite cool, about -0.9Z. The forecast for the difference between the west and east temperatures, the IOD index (Figure 2C), is actually the warmest since 1982, though the corresponding 80thpercentile confidence intervals are quite broad, ranging from -1.3Z to +3Z. The circle colors in Figure 2 denote wet, normal and dry SOND rainfall outcomes in Eastern East Africa. Strong positive IOD forecasts (Figure 2C) are clearly associated with very frequent wet rainy seasons.

**Section 4. JAS El Niño-based analogs-based rainfall probabilities for Ethiopia/South Sudan and southern Sudan**

Figure 3 presents the chance of JAS rainfall being below-normal or very dry based on 1982-2022 strong El Niño years. These El Niño years, identified in Figure 1A, contain several very dry and several moderately dry Ethiopian JAS seasons. The x and y values of the red 2023 circle are based on the 2023 NMME Niño3.4 SST forecast and a regression-based Ethiopian rainfall value. The regression was based on 1982-2022 observed Niño3.4 SST and regionally-averaged CHIRPS SPI values (R2=0.52).

Figure 3B suggests that much of Ethiopia may have a 50-to-80% chance of experiencing below normal JAS rains, and a similar historical shift in the odds appears over South Sudan and Southern Sudan. Using a 1-in-5 year threshold to identify ‘very dry’ seasons, we also find 40-70% chances of such an outcome over many parts of these countries (Figure 3C). It should be noted that the agro-climatic context across, and within, these countries is complicated. Rainfall deficits in more arid regions will likely be more hazardous. In Ethiopia, recent rains have been above-normal in many areas, and soil moisture estimates for June indicate above normal moisture levels (Figure 4). This will likely help mitigate the impact of JAS rainfall deficits. In 2023, La Niña conditions transitioned quickly into an El Niño, and the MAM Belg season received copious rains. This can be contrasted with more perilous conditions in 2015, in which El Niño conditions were well-established the previous year, and 2015 Belg rains were poor. There have been seasons, however, like JAS 2002 and 2009, in which El Niño developed rapidly following a La Niña, and poor JAS rains contributed to serious food insecurity (2002 FEWS NET[report](https://fews.net/sites/default/files/documents/reports/Ethiopia_200409en.pdf)and 2009 FEWS NET[report](https://fews.net/sites/default/files/documents/reports/Ethiopia_200409en.pdf)).

In addition to potential crop moisture deficits, conflict and poor access to seeds and fertilizer in Ethiopia, South Sudan and Sudan are also likely to negatively impact both short and long-cycled crop productivity.

**Section 5. El Niño and IOD-based analogs-based rainfall probabilities for Eastern East Africa in SOND**

Figure 5 presents the chance of SOND East African rainfall being above-normal or very wet based on 1982-2022 strong El Niño years and/or strong positive Indian Ocean years. There is a strong propensity for El Niños and positive IOD events to co-evolve. The x and y values of the red 2023 circle are based on the 2023 NMME Niño3.4 and IOD forecasts.

Within these analogs seasons, most of Eastern East Africa sees a strong increase in the frequency of above-normal rainy seasons and very wet rainy seasons (Figure 5B,C). Very wet seasons are 1-in-5 year (80th percentile) rainfall seasons. Dynamic models tend to represent rains well in this region and season, and results (Figure 6) from NOAA’s new Hybrid Prediction (Dynamical and Machine Learning Models) System ([here](https://psl.noaa.gov/forecasts/s2s_fewsnet/)) also indicate a very high probability of very wet rainfall outcomes. In many areas of eastern Kenya, southern Somalia and parts of Ethiopia these probabilities exceed 80%.

It seems likely that this region will experience heavy rainfall in late 2023. As in prior heavy rainy seasons, the impacts of these precipitation events will be complicated. Pasture conditions and water availability will improve, and agricultural opportunities will generally expand. But hazards associated with flooding, pests, locusts, and other hazards associated with extreme rains will also likely increase.

**Section 6. NDJF El Niño-based analogs for Zimbabwe in NDJF**

Figure 7 presents the chance of NDJF rainfall in Zimbabwe being below-normal or very dry based on 1982-2022 strong El Niño years. While there have been strong El Niño years with normal Zimbabwe rainfall outcomes (i.e. 1997/98 and 2009/10), six years had poor rainfall outcomes (1982/83, 1986/87, 1991/92, 1994/95, 2002/03, 2015/16). The x and y values of the red 2023 circle are based on the 2023 NMME Niño3.4 SST forecast and a regression-based Zimbabwe SPI estimate. The regression was based on 1982-2022 observed Niño3.4 SST and nationally-averaged CHIRPS SPI values (R2=0.47).

Figure 7B suggests that much of Zimbabwe may have a 50-to-80% chance of experiencing below normal NDJF rains. Composites indicating the probability of very dry outcomes (Figure 7C) is particularly concerning, with key agricultural areas in central and eastern Zimbabwe potentially facing a 70%-plus chance of a 1-in-5 year dry season. Zimbabwe faces very high levels of inflation.
