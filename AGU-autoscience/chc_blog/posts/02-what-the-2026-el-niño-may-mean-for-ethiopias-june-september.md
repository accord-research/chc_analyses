# What the 2026 El Niño may mean for Ethiopia’s June – September Kiremt Rainy Season

Authors: Chris Funk, Laura Harrison, Asaminew Teshome, Diriba Korecha, Chris Shitote, Weston Anderson, Hussen Seid and Masilin Gudoshava

**Key Messages:**

1) El Niño events are historically associated with suppression of Kiremt (Jun-Sep) rainfall over central, northern and northeastern Ethiopia, often leading to below-normal seasonal rainfall and increased drought risk.

2) Current oceanic and atmospheric observations and global climate model forecasts indicate a fully established El Niño by July or earlier.

3) Recent consolidated forecasts from the Ethiopian Meteorological Institute and the ICPAC[call](https://www.icpac.net/documents/1076/Technical_Statement_from_the_73rd_Greater_Horn_of_Africa_Climate_Outlook_Forum_GnOzTFx.pdf)for high chances of below-normal rains in northern, northeastern and central Ethiopia during the JJAS 2026 season.

4) The combined influence of El Niño conditions and the forecast of above-normal air temperatures is expected to substantially suppress Ethiopia’s critical Kiremt rainfall and reduce water availability during JJAS 2026 across much of central, northern and northeastern Ethiopia.

5) Reduced Kiremt rainfall could adversely affect crop production, pasture availability and water resources, particularly in rain-fed agricultural areas of central, northern and northeastern Ethiopia.

**Introduction**

Current forecasts expect the rapid development of El Niño conditions during May to July 2026. According to the mid-May 2026 assessment by the NOAA Climate Prediction Center (CPC), there is more than a 90% chance of an El Niño event by June-July-August (JJA). Despite this fast transition, the event may be weak during the start of the Kiremt season and increase in strength as the season progresses, potentially to  a moderate or strong event by July to September. Forecasts indicate increasing strength through the rest of 2026.

Given recent below-normal JJAS 2026 rainfall outlooks from the Ethiopian Meteorological Institute and the IGAD Climate Prediction and Applications Centre (73rd Greater Horn of Africa Climate Outlook Forum ([link](https://www.icpac.net/documents/1076/Technical_Statement_from_the_73rd_Greater_Horn_of_Africa_Climate_Outlook_Forum_GnOzTFx.pdf)) and strong historical links between El Niño events and dry conditions during the June-July-August-September (JJAS) ‘Kiremt’ rainy seasons, this analysis employs high resolution climate data and historical forecasts to illustrate why there is need for concern about Kiremt 2026.

Forecasts indicate there are moderate-to-high probabilities of below-normal JJAS 2026 season rainfall and above-normal temperatures. The pessimistic outlooks are largely due to El Niño conditions, which are developing and are forecast to strengthen during JJAS2026. Co-occurring rainfall deficits and above-average temperatures could exacerbate vegetation impacts during dry periods, depending on when and where these materialize.

Here, we focus on several sources of information: The observed tendencies of JJAS rainfall in Ethiopia during past El Niño events, using post 1981 El Niño years identified from Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI) [data](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso/roni/) and high resolution CHIRPS v3 rainfall data ([webpage](https://chc.ucsb.edu/data/chirps3), Scientific Data [Paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-026-07096-4)), and a dynamical climate model forecast for JJAS 2026. For a large region of central-northern Ethiopia that exhibits a relatively strong ENSO-rainfall teleconnection, we examine the historical and current JJAS rainfall forecasts from the ECMWF SEAS5 model, which is one of the more skillful dynamical climate models for that region and season. Additionally, we look to the historical ENSO-temperature outcomes, using CHIRTS-ERA5 Tmax data, to show that with El Niño come the likely outcomes of a hotter and drier-than-normal JJAS season. Current model forecasts for JJAS 2026 are predicting very high chances of much higher-than-average temperatures during this upcoming season. When El Niño-driven dry Kiremt seasons have occurred in the past, they have brought a combination of elevated temperatures, increased atmospheric evaporative demand and suppressed rainfall. Unlike La Niña droughts in southern Ethiopia during the Belg season, which tend to affect drier areas, these dry conditions can impact densely populated humid regions in central and northern Ethiopia. The predicted moderate-to-strong El Niño and concurrent extreme temperatures during the JJAS 2026 season are forecasted to severely suppress Ethiopia’s critical Kiremt rainfall, driving a more than 50% probability of below-normal rainfall across central and northern regions. This destructive combination of severe moisture deficits and accelerated evapotranspiration poses critical risks of widespread agricultural crop stress, water scarcity, and food insecurity.

**El Niño and Kiremt rainfall**

El Niño is the warm phase of the El Niño-Southern Oscillation, a recurring global-scale climate mode that involves anomalous oceanic and atmospheric conditions that reinforce each other and create predictable disruptions to seasonal weather patterns. These originate in the tropical Pacific Ocean during a sustained period of warmer-than-normal sea surface temperatures in the central-eastern equatorial region, and propagate globally through various atmospheric wave patterns and greater warmth across much of the tropical troposphere. The regional climate impacts during Ethiopia’s JJAS (Kiremt) season are typically marked by highly variable rainfall distributions, where the western and southwestern areas benefit from adequate seasonal rains while the northern, central, northeastern and eastern parts face moisture-related stress. This variability, particularly in the western and southwestern regions, often manifests as localized heavy downpours and flooding, while in northern, northeastern, central, and eastern parts of the country may experience delayed onset, uneven spatial and temporal distribution, or early cessation of rains. These conditions commonly contribute to moisture stress, declining agricultural productivity, and increased risks of food insecurity.

El Niño usually suppresses Kiremt rains, often leading to below-normal rains or drought over much of northeastern and eastern Ethiopia, and parts of central Ethiopia. Examples of El Niño events during the June to September season, ‘analog years’, include 1982, 1987, 1991, 1994, 1997, 2002, 2004, 2015, and 2023, based on Relative Oceanic Niño Index (Relative ONI, RONI) values from [NOAA CPC](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso/roni/). These years had RONI values of at least +0.5 °C in both JJA and JAS. 1993 was excluded because it was a year in which El Niño was tapering down, not building up over the Kiremt season.

Relative ONI was recently adopted by several official international climate monitoring agencies as the primary ENSO monitoring index. This index uses the relative differences between central-eastern equatorial Pacific Niño 3.4 sea surface temperatures (SST) and the global tropical mean SST and a statistical adjustment. Rainfall outcomes during JJAS El Niño analog years can be viewed at the Early Warning eXplorer ([EWX](https://earlywarning.usgs.gov/fews/ewx/index.html?region=af)) or at the Ethiopian Meteorological Institute ([https://www.ethiomet.gov.et/](https://www.ethiomet.gov.et/)).

The composite response for the El Niño Kiremt (JJAS) season rains are shown in Figure 1. These results are expressed as the frequency of below-normal rains. Much of Ethiopia, as well as Uganda, western Kenya, South Sudan, southern Sudan and Yemen are expected to have a high (>50%) chance of below-normal rainfall during JJAS. In Ethiopia, many of these areas also correspond with regions of dense population (inset in Figure 1). In northeast Ethiopia, and parts of northwest Ethiopia, these probabilities exceed 70%. The central-northern Ethiopia region of focus (7°–14°N, 36.5°– 40.5°E) was previously examined following the high-impact 2015 El Niño-driven drought that impacted the Belg and Kiremt seasons ([Funk et al., 2016](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/bams/97/12/bams-d-16-0167.1.xml)). This region was selected based on robust El Niño teleconnections and relatively high rainfall during the Kiremt season.

The left panel of Figure 2 shows a scatter plot of observed JAS RONI values and observed CHIRPS v3 rainfall, in the target rectangle drawn in Figure 1. The selected analog years are identified in red. The RONI values range from +0.8°C to +2.0°C. The associated Kiremt rainfall totals range from above-average to very low amounts, with most strong El Niño years exhibiting much lower rainfall than average. The link between 1981-2025 average rainfall and RONI is quite high (R2=0.5). The El Niño-Southern Oscillation accounts for about half the year-to-year variability in average Kiremt rainfall in this region. But it is worth noting that much of this predictive power comes from moderate-to-strong El Niño and La Niña years. Weak El Niño years (with RONI values of less than +1 °C) appear quite similar to normal years. Moderate or stronger events (with RONI values greater than +1 °C) appear to have a consistent dry signal. As shown in the right panel of Figure 1, current (May 2026) CPC forecasts indicate that in JJA 2026, a weak El Niño is the most likely outcome. During the Kiremt season, the El Niño is forecast to strengthen and by JAS 2026, a moderate-to-strong event is most likely (66% chance). These forecasts will be updated, and it will be important to consider both the weak and moderate-to-strong scenarios. The speed at which the El Niño develops will be an important determinant of risk. Some seasons, for example 1994, had weak El Niño conditions and very wet rains. Overall, however, six of the nine El Niño seasons had seasonal rainfall amounts that fell in the bottom 20% of rainy seasons, with totals of less than 782 mm.

The spatial fidelity of model-based JJAS forecasts, their past successes at correctly predicting locations of below-normal rainfall, and their ability to accurately predict locations of extreme JJAS rainfall varies. In central-northern Ethiopia, there is a strong historical El Niño-rainfall relationship, and forecasts from the ECMWF SEAS5 model have generally performed well ([Ehsan et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00382-021-05855-0)). Figure 3 shows a bar plot of ECMWF JJAS forecasts (May initial conditions) in central-northern Ethiopia. The ensemble mean forecast was correct 75 percent of the time when forecasting below-normal JJAS rainfall forecasts in this area during 1982-2025. The current below-average JJAS 2026 rainfall forecast (red bars in Figure 3) is similar to 1982, 1987 and 2015. While the dry moderate El Niño season in 2002 was not captured well, dry events during1983, 1987, 1997,  2009, 2015, and 2023 were identified. These model results are independent of the empirical results shown in Figures 1 and 2, thereby increasing our confidence in a below-normal outlook.

**Focus on planning and preparedness for Kiremt 2026**

In central and northern Ethiopia, JJAS rainfall is critically important to agriculture, environment, rangeland, and hydrologic conditions, as the majority of annual rainfall occurs during those months. The JJAS season contributes 60-to-70% of the annual total rainfall over major parts of Ethiopia, and up to 85% in northwestern regions. Ethiopia’s main Meher crop production season strongly depends on the Kiremt season rains. In the northwest, these rains are typically high, while rains are usually less consistent in the less humid central-northern highlands. In general, lower elevation areas will be drier, and tend to be impacted more. This may explain why yield data from sorghum and maize, which are often grown at lower elevations, appear to be more negatively affected than wheat, which is typically grown at higher elevations.

The July to September Karma season rains critically support pastoralist livelihoods in Ethiopia’s northeastern Afar region. JJAS rainfall also supports the latter part of the ‘long cycle’ growing seasons in Ethiopia’s central-southern highland regions. These slowly maturing crops can be highly productive, but may be negatively impacted by poor Kiremt rains.

During past El Niño years, there have been frequent below-normal Meher season sorghum and maize yields. Based on historical FAO crop yield data and a FEWS NET [May 2026 Extended Outlook analysis](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1HobljXHisZhLy9ucJeB3plCHl2FndkIM/edit?slide=id.g3e302d01921_0_2461#slide=id.g3e302d01921_0_2461), yields have typically been more depressed in eastern Amhara, Tigray, and northeastern Oromia, while less severe yield reductions have occurred in western Ethiopia. At a national scale, yield outcomes have varied and overall have been slightly below average (2% below average) but some cases resulted in more substantial deficits of 10% to 15% below average. Note that these results are based on composites for El Niño years, and do not take into account current conditions. The Belg rains in 2026 can be contrasted with the poor Belg season in 2015. While March 2026 rainfall was highly above average, April to May 2026 rainfall was below average across much of southern and central Ethiopia. Subseasonal forecasts, from June 2nd, indicate drier-than-average conditions in northern and western East Africa in June 2026. During 2015, rains were poor in both the Belg and Kiremt seasons. This resulted in nationwide catastrophes, a substantial reduction in crop yields, dried-up water sources, and a scarcity of pasture across the eastern half of Ethiopia, including the Rift Valley regions.

An additional hazard to look out for in 2026 will be abnormally high temperatures. These dry out soils more quickly and increase the risks of crop heat stress, pests, and disease. Above-normal JJAS maximum temperatures tend to occur during El Niños, concurrent with less rainy conditions (Figure 4). Across the region, climate models predict that JJAS 2026 temperatures will be much hotter than most JJAS seasons, and with high odds of surpassing 90th percentile temperatures (Figure 4 inset).

In-season monitoring of agroclimatic conditions and forecasts will be important for timely identification, due to the severe impacts that abnormally low or irregular rainfall can have on rainfed crop production and pastoral resources, and especially on livelihoods in semi arid and arid zones. Figure 5 and Table 1 map and list of woredas representing the areas identified by the **Ethiopian Disaster Risk Management Commission** ([EDRMC](https://edrmc.gov.et/about-us/edrmc)) as potentially vulnerable to **El Niño-related impacts**. These areas are expected to experience varying degrees of JJAS seasonal rainfall-related stresses, including below-normal rainfall, moisture deficits, and associated agricultural and livelihood disruptions, depending on the severity and spatial distribution of the El Niño event.
