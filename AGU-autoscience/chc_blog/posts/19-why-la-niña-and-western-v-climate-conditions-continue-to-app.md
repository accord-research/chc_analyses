# Why La Niña and “Western V” Climate Conditions Continue to Appear Conducive to Poor Eastern East Africa March-to-May 2021 Rains and Crop Production

**Why La Niña and “Western V” Climate Conditions Continue to Appear Conducive to Poor Eastern East Africa March-to-May 2021 Rains and Crop Production**

Chris Funk, Gideon Galu, Andrew Hoell, Laura Harrison, Diriba Korecha, Donghoon Lee, Shrad Shukla, Chris Shitote, Frank Davenport, Will Turner, Diego Pedreros, Juliet Way-Henthorne, and Greg Husak

**Overview**

Building on a series of blogs that began last June, we provide yet another update on the outlook for the upcoming March-to-May 2021 rains. This post will have four sections. The first section will briefly update our statistical forecasts and analog seasons, based on current (February) sea surface temperature conditions. The second section will examine recent (February) reanalysis fields to 1) explain why Pacific Ocean sea surface temperatures can drive predictable March-to-May droughts and 2) underscore that the observed climate anomalies appear similar to prior droughts in many recent dry seasons.

The third section will examine Water Requirement Satisfaction Index (WRSI) simulations and yield statistics, for analog seasons, to begin assessing potential agricultural impacts.

The fourth section will briefly examine recent CHIRPS data, downscaled CHIRPS-GEFS weather forecasts, and the latest Subseasonal Experiment (SubX) multi-model rainfall predictions.

**Updated Sea Surface Temperature Anomalies**

Standardized February 2021 sea surface temperature (SST) anomalies appear similar to prior conditions, with cool La Niña conditions prevalent in the central equatorial Pacific Ocean, juxtaposed with exceptionally warm waters in the “Western V” region, stretching from Indonesia northeast into the extratropical Pacific. Indian Ocean SSTs are slightly below normal off the coast of Somalia—and exceptionally warm off the southeastern coast of Southern Africa. Based on[prior research](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=790), the CHC/NOAA PSD science team has identified eight recent seasons with similar sea surface temperature anomalies: 1999, 2000, 2001, 2008, 2009, 2011, 2012, and 2017. This list includes all the recent years with a strong “Western V” gradient (WVG), which can be defined as the difference between average sea surface temperatures in the NINO4 region, minus the average sea surface temperatures in the Western V. These areas are shown in Figure 1. The WVG is an updated version of the “West Pacific Gradient,” first defined by FEWS NET scientists in[2013](https://doi.org/10.1175/JCLI-D-12-00344.1). The WVG adds the northern Pacific. The “climate response” section below will describe why we believe warming in this region reinforces the strength of La Niña events.

Figure 2 displays sea surface temperatures for the analog seasons. Figure 3 displays a simple univariate regression-based forecast for March-to-May rains in the eastern East African region (Kenya, Somalia, and Ethiopia south and east of 38°E, 8°N). Note that this scatterplot describes a nonlinear relationship. When the central-eastern Pacific is neutral or warmer than normal, towards the right-hand side of the scatterplot, there is little predictability. On the other hand, as you move to the left of Figure 3, you find a distinct shift towards drier March-to-May outcomes. The regression forecast for 2021 is ~-0.8Z, but with a large cross-validated standard error (~1Z). Assuming this distribution, there would be a 62% chance of below-normal rainfall (<-0.5Z),  a 28% chance of normal rainfall (±0.5Z), and a 10% chance of above-normal rainfall (>0.5Z).

Figure 4 displays Eastern East Africa rainfall data back to 1900, using a combination of Centennial Trends interpolated gauge data (1900-1980) and CHIRPS (1981-2020), along with the 2021**regression forecast**of -0.8Z. Note that the Centennial Trends and CHIRPS data are highly correlated (r=0.93).

**Examining February Reanalysis fields during analog seasons and 2021**

We next turn to a diagnostic discussion, exploring why sea surface temperature fields like those shown in Figures 1 and 2 are very often associated with below-normal Eastern East African rains. While we can express our forecast via a set of analogs or a simple regression, it is just as important to understand the physical links between these patterns and the associated circulation disruptions. This understanding helps overcome issues of non-stationarity in a warming climate. It also helps us see if the atmosphere is responding in ways that are likely to produce dry conditions over the eastern Horn. We begin by looking at upper level (200 hPa) geopotential heights and wind fields for analogs years (Figures 5 and 6) from the NCEP/NCAR Reanalysis dataset. These images were obtained from the NOAA Physical Sciences Laboratory (psl.noaa.gov). In Figure 5, we see over the equatorial eastern Pacific characteristic La Niña height anomalies: an upper-level low-pressure cell characteristic of a Matsuno-Gill response to equatorial diabatic forcing near the Maritime Continent, with negative height anomalies of around -60 m. To the north of this upper-level low, near the area of warm extratropical sea surface temperatures shown in Figure 2, we find above normal upper-level height anomalies of about +80 m. This response is related to both the direct influence of warm under-lying sea surface temperatures and the non-local response to increased precipitation and diabatic forcing near Indonesia. Between this high and low, we find a strong westward wind anomaly, which turns towards the equator. Figure 6 shows the associated wind speeds and wind vectors. The anomalous winds reach speeds of more than -11 m/s, then curve towards the equator from the north and south, converging near the equator (~180°E/W). This convergence produces dry subsiding air, suppressing rainfall near the dateline, and helping to intensify La Niña impacts. Looking at equatorial vertical velocity anomalies during analogs (Figure 7), we see substantial (± 2 to 3 Pa/second) anomalies associated with increases in sinking near the dateline and increases in rising motions near the Maritime Continent.

Using this baseline, we can examine 2021, and ask ourselves how well it conforms to this pattern. Figures 8, 9, and 10 display the corresponding high, wind, and vertical velocity anomalies for February 2021. In each case, we see a response substantially stronger than the analog seasons. While the equatorial La Niña upper-level low is similar to the set of analog years (~-50 m), the high-pressure cell in the northern Pacific is much higher, more than +200 m! We have noted the importance of similar responses in our examinations of the 2017 March-to-May drought ([here](https://rmets.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/qj.3266) and [here](https://doi.org/10.1175/BAMS-D-18-0108.1)). While this result seems quite extreme, upper-level height fields are captured well by models, and we find a very compelling convergence between four separate reanalyses in this region (Figure 11). These large positive north Pacific anomalies converge with low equatorial values to drive wind anomalies (Figure 8) that are substantially larger than the average anomaly responses (Figure 6). Between the high and low in Figure 6, we find westerly wind anomalies of ~-11 m/s; the corresponding values in Figure 8 are more than -20 m/s. This is directly related to the north-south geopotential height gradient, and the extremely high north Pacific heights (Figure 11). Basic atmospheric dynamics tell us that these wind speeds should scale with the geopotential height gradient. These winds ultimately converge over the equatorial central Pacific, producing sinking motions.

By contrasting Figures 7 and 10, we can get a relative sense of the magnitude of the La Niña-related disruption in the equatorial Walker Circulation. Near the Maritime Continent, in the analogs (Figure 7) we find vertical velocity anomalies on the order of -0.02 Pa/second. In Figure 10, we find anomalies less than -0.03 and -0.04 Pa/second. This indicates a strong enhancement of the Walker Circulation, even relative to the selected anomalies. Furthermore, looking along the equator at 55-65°E, we find equally large downward velocities, indicating strong subsidence just off the coast of East Africa, which could migrate onshore in March, April, and/or May. This subsidence pattern is not present in the analog velocities plotted in Figure 7, which again suggests the 2021 conditions appear troubling, even in comparison to the selected analogs.

**Summary**– The February reanalysis fields appear to have all the key circulation features associated with a strong Western V gradient La Niña. Even in the context of these recent strong gradient seasons, the 2021 circulation features appear very conducive to a very strong La Niña-like response. This reinforces that the chosen analogs appear appropriate and that a worst-case, very severe drought (similar perhaps to 1999, 2000, 2009, and 2011) is certainly plausible.

**Crop Outcomes for Analog Seasons**

While obviously a detailed projection of crop outcomes is implausible at this point, we can use our set of analog seasons to explore potential crop outcomes. We do this by analyzing time-series of historic crop Water Requirement Satisfaction Index (WRSI) simulations. We also examine maize crop yield statistics for Kenya.

To assess the risk of a below-normal crop water stress season, we used our set of analogs to assess the probability of an end-of-season WRSI outcome falling within the bottom 33% of the historical distribution. Figure 12 displays these results. The map on the left shows that below-normal maize water satisfaction outcomes for much of Kenya and Somalia are likely, i.e., given the analogs, there appears to be more than a 50% chance of a 1-in-3 year dry outcome. The WRSI analogs for the Belg season (right-hand map) appear even more concerning. Many areas could have more than a 70% chance of ending up with bottom tercile WRSI values.

Using data from the FEWS NET Data Warehouse, we also examined yield performance in Kenya during our analog seasons (Figure 13). This data ends in 2016, so 2017 was not included. Uncertainties in the yield data and the WRSI simulations result in local differences between these sources of information,**and these results are not intended to inform localized hazards assessments. Nevertheless, on a regional scale, these analyses indicate that below-normal agricultural outcomes appear likely over substantial portions of Kenya, Somalia, and the Belg growing regions in Ethiopia.**

**Current Early Estimates and Model Forecasts**

Finally, we close with an examination of the most recent Early Estimates for March and the most recent SubX forecasts from the CHC, the SubX weekly forecasts[from March 11th](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/shrad/SubX/Plots/Archive/20210311/E_Africa/pr_sfc_E_Africa_IC_20210311.png). Weekly updates are available[here](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/shrad/SubX/Plots/Archive/Latest/E_Africa/). According to the March 1 to March 25th Early Estimates (Figure 14), March may be exceptionally dry across eastern Tanzania, almost all of Kenya, and much of Ethiopia. On average, however, March is still very dry across much of this region, so the associated anomalies in millimeters are not particularly large. However, this map does suggest that this region is experiencing dry sinking air (subsidence) and below normal atmospheric moisture, suggesting that we are seeing the expected slow start to the rainy season. SubX forecasts for the next four weeks tell a similar story, although, again, the magnitude of the anomalies are small.

CHIRPS-GEFS forecasts and our analog analyses indicate a slow start to the season and below-normal rainfall outcomes in many regions. Figure 15 examines the start of the season and the establishment of the March-May rains in two areas – Kenya’s south-east Lowlands (A) and Somalia’s Bay sorghum producing region (B).

For the south-east Kenya Lowlands (Figure 15A), observations and CHIRPS-GEFS forecasts indicate very low rainfall totals through March, indicating slightly delayed onset and planting dates. Filling out the rest of the season using mean analog rainfall data suggests a peak in April, but with totals that are about half the 2000-2018 average. Both the analogs and the 2000-2018 average indicate that the season essentially ends at the beginning of May. Thus, the season will likely have a short growing period (~1.5 months). A late start and poor April rains will likely lead to below-average yields or crop failure.

For the Somalia Bay sorghum production region, it also appears likely that there will be a delayed start of the season and late planting. The analog years suggest substantial rainfall deficits in April, followed by a timely cessation in May. As in Kenya, the season will likely have a short growing period (~1.5 months), and a late start and poor April rains will likely lead to below-average yields or crop failure.

A recent paper has also shown that[A slow rainy season onset is a reliable harbinger of drought in most food-insecure regions in Sub-Saharan Africa](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0242883). In a month, we will have much more information as to the likely outcome for food-insecure East Africa. But for now, the results presented here suggest that maintaining a high degree of concern appears warranted.
