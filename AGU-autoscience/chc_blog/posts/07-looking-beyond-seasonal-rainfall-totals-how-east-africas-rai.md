# Looking Beyond Seasonal Rainfall Totals: How East Africa’s Rainy Seasons Are Changing

[](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Dry_corn_field.jpg)

![](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Dry_corn_field-300x200.jpg)

Author: Daniella Alaso

Farmers have long relied on the seasonal rhythm of the rains to guide planting decisions, manage livestock, and prepare for the growing season. But in recent decades, that rhythm has become increasingly uncertain.

A new[study](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/hydr/27/3/JHM-D-25-0080.1.xml)by researchers from the University of Florida, the University of Leeds, and the University of California Santa Barbara Climate Hazards Center reveals that rainy seasons across East Africa have been changing over the last four decades. While some areas are experiencing longer and wetter rainy seasons, others are becoming shorter and drier. Just as importantly, the drivers behind these changes vary across the region.

“Changes in the timing and duration of the rainy seasons have significant implications for agriculture and livelihoods,” said co-author[Dr. Chris Funk](https://www.chc.ucsb.edu/people/chris-funk), research director of UCSB’s Climate Hazards Center. “For many regions dependent on rainfed agriculture, decisions on when to plant, apply fertilizer, and manage crops depend on the reliable rhythm of the seasonal rains. When that rhythm changes, it can have cascading effects on agricultural production and food security.”

To better understand these shifts, the researchers examined more than 40 years of daily rainfall data from the CHIRPS dataset, investigating changes in the timing, duration, frequency, and intensity of rainfall events across East Africa.

What emerged was a complex picture. In unimodal areas of northern East Africa, including Sudan and northwestern Ethiopia, wetter conditions are primarily being driven by an increase in the number of rainy days.

Further south, the story changes. Across parts of southern East Africa, including Tanzania, rainy seasons have also become wetter, but for a different reason. Here, increases in seasonal rainfall are being driven primarily by more intense rainfall events rather than more rainy days.

“This is an important distinction, said lead-author[Jesse Kisembe](https://geog.ufl.edu/directory/jesse-kisembe/), a graduate student at the University of Florida, Department of Geography. A wetter season can arise because it rains harder, because it rains more often, or some combination of the two. Those differences matter because rainfall spread across frequent moderate events can have very different impacts than rainfall concentrated in a few intense storms.” While additional rainfall can benefit agriculture and water resources, intense downpours are more likely to increase the risk of flooding, soil erosion, and infrastructure damage.

Concerning changes were also observed in the eastern Horn of Africa, including parts of eastern Kenya and central and southern Somalia. “The March to May rains are becoming shorter and less reliable in many parts of the eastern Horn,” said co-author[Dr. Caroline Wainwright](https://environment.leeds.ac.uk/see/staff/12868/dr-caroline-wainwright), Climate Science Lecturer at the University of Leeds. For decades, scientists have documented a decline in the region’s March to May long rains. This new study builds on that work by examining rainfall characteristics in recent decades and using location-specific seasonality in rainfall instead of fixed calendar months.“We’re seeing fewer rainy days and, in many areas, an earlier end to the season. This means a shorter window for crops to develop and mature.” For the few areas with changes towards an earlier onset of rains, an earlier end to the season generally outweighed any gains from an earlier start, resulting in a shorter March to May rainy season.

“Long-term satellite and rainfall datasets now allow us to move beyond simply measuring whether regions are getting wetter or drier,” said corresponding author[Dr. Yixin Wen](https://geog.ufl.edu/directory/dr-berry-wen/), Assistant Professor of Meteorology and AI at the University of Florida. “We can identify how rainfall characteristics are changing at local scales and better understand the risks these changes pose to agriculture, water resources, and food security.” This type of information is essential for developing more effective climate adaptation and early warning strategies.

“Shifts in rainfall characteristics are of particular interest to the Climate Hazards Center and its partners,” said Dr. Chris Funk. “Information on when rains begin and end, and how rainfall is distributed throughout the season, helps partners such as the Famine Early Warning Systems Network ([FEWS NET](https://fews.net/)), national meteorological agencies, and ministries of agriculture, anticipate agricultural outcomes and assess potential food security impacts.”

For many farmers and pastoralists in East Africa, the impacts of seasonal rainfall characteristics  are not abstract; they shape harvests, incomes, and access to food. A[failed](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/?p=1905)2025 October to December season, followed by below-average and poorly distributed rainfall during the 2026 March to May (Gu) season, has heightened concerns about food security conditions in Somalia. Humanitarian agencies, including FEWS NET, have[warned](https://fews.net/east-africa/somalia/key-message-update/may-2026)that a late onset and erratic Gu rains will likely contribute to a second consecutive season of failed or below-average crops in key agropastoral areas of Bay and Bakool, where Emergency outcomes (Phase 4 in the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification System, IPC) are expected with credible risks of Famine (IPC Phase 5) if harvests fail.
