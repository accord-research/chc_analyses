# Southern Africa hit with driest February on record in central areas

By Laura Harrison, Chris Funk, Will Turner, Tamuka Magadzire, Andrew Hoell, Pete Peterson, Shrad Shukla, Pamella Mogane, and Greg Husak

A severe and prolonged dry spell in late January to February 2024 triggered deficits in[root zone soil moisture](https://ldas.gsfc.nasa.gov/fldas/models/forecast),[streamflow](https://ldas.gsfc.nasa.gov/fldas/models/forecast), and water level at the[Kariba Dam](https://walkforloveafrica.org/2024/03/20/update-on-the-hydrological-outlook-at-kariba-dam/). There is potential for high impact crop stress in rainfed production areas across Southern Africa. National disasters have been declared in Zambia and Malawi.In central areas of Southern Africa, rainfall data show that this event was extreme, both in terms of its widespread area of impact and its historical rarity.

During the[past two months](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/products/CHIRPS-2.0/moving_12pentad/pngs/africa_southern/archive/PON_12PentAccum_2024_p15.png), many areas across Southern Africa received only half of their typical rainfall, or less, according to preliminary CHIRPS data.[Seasonal rainfall totals](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/products/Season_Monitor/africa_southern/oct_to_apr/pngs/with_forecast/archive/SPI_36PentAccum_2024_p18.png)are below average and rains typically taper off by April. Forecast below-average rainfall in late March suggests few opportunities for improved seasonal totals. Conditions like these can harshly impact livelihoods dependent on rainfed agriculture and productive grazing lands,[constrain energy supplies](https://www.greenbuildingafrica.co.za/kariba-dam-water-allocation-for-electricity-generation-cut-by-almost-half-in-2024/), and force higher food prices.

In this article, we highlight important factors driving the extreme dry spell and explain how warnings about El Niño impacts in Southern Africa gave very early indications for 2023-24 drought conditions.

**Record low rainfall during a crucial time**

February 2024 was the driest February on the 40+ year[CHIRPS](https://chc.ucsb.edu/data/chirps)data record, for a large area spanning southeastern Angola, southern Zambia, western, central, and northern Zimbabwe, northeastern Namibia, and northern and eastern Botswana (Figure 1-left).

![Figure 1. Left – February 2024 CHIRPS rainfall totals over central Southern Africa were the driest of the past four decades in many locations (dark red), including in major crop production areas in Zambia and Zimbabwe. The black box denotes central Southern Africa (20E-35E, 13S-22.5S). Right – A composite time-series of February central Southern Africa rainfall totals from the Global Precipitation Climatology Centre (GPCC, 1920-1980) and CHIRPS (1981-2024). During their period of overlap (1981-2020), these two time-series are very similar (r=0.92).](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Feb-2024-rank-map-and-historical-timeseries-for-large-impacted-area.png)

The extreme dry spell began in late January 2024 and lasted for over a month, spanning a time when seasonal rains would normally be nearing their peak and when rainfall is of critical importance to maize crop production. During this time,plants need ample water supply to complete vegetative growth, then produce and begin to fill cobs.

Such a dry February is rare for central Southern Africa. Using a 1920 to 2024 time series of rainfall averaged for this region constructed from CHIRPS satellite-gauge and Global Precipitation Climatology Centre gridded gauge data (Figure 1-right), February 2024 stands out as extreme. It is rivaled only by the extreme low rainfall in February 1992– which occurred during another strong El Niño-driven drought. Similarities in regional atmospheric and oceanic drivers in the extreme dry February of 2024 and 1992 are highlighted in this article.

**Double trouble: Hot and dry**

Daytime maximum temperatures (Figure 2) were much higher than average across central and southern Southern Africa in[late 2023](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/CHIRTS-ERA5/tmax/regional_summary/Southern_Africa/Tmax.summary_SouthernAfr_2023_01Nov-22Dec_CHIRTS-ERA5.daily_Tmax.png)and[throughout the dry spell](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/experimental/CHIRTS-ERA5/tmax/regional_summary/SubSaharanAfrica/Tmax.summary_SubSaharanAfrica_2024_21Jan-28Feb_CHIRTS-ERA5.daily_Tmax.png). The season began with challenges for many rainfed farmers, when dry soils and poor rains in[November 2023](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/products/CHIRPS-2.0/moving_06pentad/pngs/africa_southern/archive/Anomaly_06PentAccum_2023_p66.png)forced many central region farmers to plant[up to a month later than usual](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/will/Season_WRSI_Monitoring/Southern_Africa/Maize/CurrentSOSAnomaly.png). These were followed by good rains in[December and early January](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/products/CHIRPS-2.0/moving_06pentad/pngs/africa_southern/archive/Anomaly_06PentAccum_2024_p03.png). Then, when rains stopped in late January, hot days reportedly evaporated standing water and moisture from the soils and exacerbated crop stress (*source: personal communication in Zimbabwe; also see Figure 3*).

![Figure 2. Left – CHIRTS-ERA5 Tmax anomalies for Jan 21st to March 7th, 2024. Stippling denotes locations warmer than 95th percentile, using a 1991-2020 baseline. Right – MODIS Land Surface Temperature Z-scores for February 2024, from CHC EWX.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/High-temperatures.png)

Average maximum temperatures exceeded the 95th percentile in the central region’s record-breaking dry February areas (Figure 2-left), as well as in other areas recently impacted by drier-than-average conditions, including South Africa, central Mozambique, and southern Malawi. These temperatures are estimated using the CHIRTS-ERA5 Tmax monitoring data, which uses a version of theECMWF Reanalysis v5 (ERA5)made to be interoperable with the historical satellite-station blended[CHIRTSmax](https://chc.ucsb.edu/data/chirtsmonthly).

February 2024 land surface temperatures from MODIS satellite data (Figure 2-right), converted to standardized anomalies, provide further confirmation of extreme temperatures in areas hit by the dry spell. Land surface temperatures also respond to abnormalities in heat and moisture exchange. In dark-colored areas of Figure 2 (left and right) high temperatures are likely driven by warm, sunny, dry conditions and by constraints on evaporation and plant transpiration. High temperatures can themselves prevent grain formation and reduce grain quality. For maize, too little moisture and high temperatures is a hazardous combination for yields. During the 2024 season, rainfed farming operations were especially vulnerable to maize yield reductions (Figure 3-left) and crop failures (Figure 3-right).

The farmer reported crop failure due to the extended dry spell and late onset of rains. 30 miles south of Mutare, Zimbabwe. February 28th, 2024 (photo credit: Laura Harrison/UCSB CHC).](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/photos-malawi-cob-and-zimbabwe-farm-visit-small.png)

**Atmospheric drivers: Why was it so bad?**

The extreme dry conditions can be attributed to very strong regional atmospheric anomalies driven by El Niño and the Subtropical Indian Ocean Dipole.

Rainfall reductions in central Southern Africa during El Niños are mainly caused by higher-than-normal pressure and subsidence, which inhibits vertical uplift and formation of storm conditions (Hoell et. al[2021](https://doi.org/10.1175/JCLI-D-20-0379.1)). A feature characteristic of strong, high-impact El Niños is a weaker-than-normal Angola Low (Pomposi et al.,[2018](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/aacc4c/meta)). This was the case in February 2024, and ERA5 atmospheric wind and moisture data indicate this rainfall suppression mechanism was very strong. Figure 4 shows this by comparing February 2024 moisture convergence (colors) and the direction and magnitude of moisture flow (arrows) to average February conditions.

![Figure 4. ERA5 February 2024 moisture convergence anomalies (shading) and moisture transport anomalies (arrows). The pink and green boxes mark the western and eastern regions of the Subtropical Indian Ocean Dipole (SIOD) index, which was in a strong negative phase during the dry spell.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Feb2024ERA5MoistureConvergenceAnomaliesAndTransports-1.png)

A prominent and impactful circulation feature during February 2024 was over the continent, centered near Namibia’s Caprivi Strip. Large moisture transport anomalies that follow an anticyclonic (counter clockwise) circulation path expose this as a completely weakened Angola Low.

Negative moisture convergence anomalies near this influence indicate extremely large moisture reductions, and these correspond with the epicenter of record-breaking February rainfall in southeastern Angola, southern Zambia, Zimbabwe, and northern Botswana. At a larger regional scale, negative moisture convergence anomalies were present across the expansive areas of Southern Africa where below-average February 2024 rainfall was also observed (Figure 6). Instead, abnormally high levels of moisture were focused into eastern Angola, northern Mozambique, and southern Tanzania. Above-average rainfall occurred in these areas.

An additional factor for the extremity of the dry spell is a low-level cyclonic circulation pattern that developed in the western subtropical Indian Ocean and prevented moisture from oceanic easterly winds from reaching into the continent. This feature is clearly visible in 850 mb wind vectors in the area of 40E to 80E, centered around 20S (Figure 5-left).

![Figure 5. Left – NCEP/NCAR Reanalysis February 2024 850 mb vector winds. Right – NOAA ERSST v5 February 2024 sea surface temperature anomalies. Black boxes mark the western and eastern regions of the Subtropical Indian Ocean Dipole (SIOD) index.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Feb-2024-850mb-wind-vectors-and-SST-anomalies.png)

The circulation appears linked to strong negative SIOD event. SIOD conditions can enhance or disrupt typical El Niño impacts in Southern Africa. December to March rainfall tends to be more negatively impacted during El Niño events with a  negative SIOD (Hoell et al.,[2017a](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00382-016-3220-6),[2017b](https://doi.org/10.1175/JHM-D-16-0294.1)). In February 2024, sea surface temperatures (SST) were much cooler than average near the western SIOD region. There was a steep gradient in cool-to-warm SST (Figure 5-right) and high-to-lower atmospheric pressure to its north and east. This maintained a disruptive pattern in which moisture flows that would ordinarily be reaching the continent were deflected northward (towards northern Madagascar) and then eastward, forming a semi-closed circulation pattern in that region. Moisture was also deflected away from the cool western SIOD region towards the south, near warmer SST anomalies.

**A memory of drought, decades old, brought back to life in 2024**

During an interview, a Zimbabwean farmer was posed with the question: Do you recall a previous year that had similar dryness and impacts as this season? His answer was enlightening. He explained that the 1992 drought was the most comparable. Like this one, for him, it had resulted in total crop failure.

Interestingly, CHIRPS data shows that 1992 used to hold the record for lowest February rains across large areas in Zimbabwe and Zambia. There is a strong likeness in the geographic rainfall pattern between this dry spell in 2024 and conditions in 1992, and some differences in central and eastern areas, too (Figure 6).

![Figure 6. Left – February 1992 CHIRPS precipitation anomaly. Right – February 2024 CHIRPS precipitation anomaly.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/CHIRPS-Feb-2024-and-1992.png)

In terms of atmospheric drivers, reanalysis data indicates that the two disruptive regional cyclonic circulations present in the 2024 dry spell were also at work in February 1992.

ERA5 moisture convergence and moisture transport anomalies for February 1992 (Figure 7) show a similar overall pattern of dryness that is prominent in central areas, but also in more of Mozambique and in southern Madagascar. Over central Southern Africa, 1992 also had a weakened Angola Low, but these data indicate that the forcing in this area, including moisture reductions into central areas and the moisture transport anomalies that identify the Low, were larger in 2024 than in 1992. This could explain why February 2024 rainfall broke the previous extreme in 1992 in these central locations.

The anomalous cyclonic circulation in the western subtropical Indian Ocean was active during the extreme February 1992, too. Like February 2024, there was a warm-cool-warm pattern present in sea surface temperature anomalies near south of Africa, southeast of Madagascar, and northeast of Madagascar in 1992 that appears to have had a role. Drier conditions in 1992 in Mozambique and southern Madagascar may have been related to a stronger, more coherent anomalous cyclonic circulation pattern in 1992.

![Figure 7. ERA5 February 1992 moisture convergence anomalies (shading) and moisture transport anomalies (arrows). The pink and green boxes mark the western and eastern regions of the Subtropical Indian Ocean Dipole (SIOD) index.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Feb1992ERA5MoistureConvergenceAnomaliesAndTransports-1.png)

Each new drought or extreme season has the potential to support forward progress in preventative actions for reducing impacts of weather and climate extremes on vulnerable populations. While these analyses have all focused on a 1-month snapshot, and thus only tell only part of a story, they provide helpful examples of damages that are possible when strong El Niño and negative SIOD-like conditions are present. With this in mind, we end with a summary of some of the techniques that we used in forecasting, monitoring, and communicating about agro climatic conditions for this 2023-24 drought season.

**Early warning for the 2023-2024 drought**

FEWS NET uses a staged early warning approach that combines long lead predictions with mid-season monitoring and weather forecasts (Funk et al.,[2019](https://doi.org/10.1175/BAMS-D-17-0233.1)). This system offered an early indication of the likelihood of below-normal rainfall conditions in Southern Africa for the 2023/24 season, and ongoing monitoring efforts provide more localized, and precise information on areal extent and severity.

In Spring 2023, climate models indicated that an El Niño would likely develop. By mid-summer NOAA PSL models and experts predicted that the then ongoing El Niño would likely build up strength to either a moderate or strong event during late 2023. Given this knowledge, scientists at the CHC used*historical analogs*(i.e., previous seasons that may look like this season), which in this case consisted of past El Niño events. Composite, analog-averages of rainfall, temperature, and yield outcomes during past El Niño events, as well dynamical climate model predictions for seasonal precipitation, air temperatures, and sea surface temperatures provided guidance of likely outcomes for the 2023-2024 season. Figure 8 shows an outlook from the CHC Blog in July 2023 that highlighted why a dry expectation for November 2023 to February 2024 rainfall during moderate to strong El Niños was strongly warranted in Southern Africa. It agrees well with areas hard-hit by drought this season.

![Figure 8. During past moderate and strong El Niños, historical data show that these areas in Southern Africa are prone to below-normal (bottom tercile) and very dry (bottom quintile) November to February rainfall. Figures are from the CHC Blog in July 2023.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/July_NMME_Rainfall_Forecasts_Zimbabwe.png)

There were many El Niño-related warnings in advance of the 2023-24 season. FEWS NET and GEOGLAM Crop Monitor for Early Warning explained to humanitarian aid organizations that they should prepare for potential drought, reduced crop harvests, exacerbation of already inflated maize prices, and heightened food insecurity in Southern Africa’s El Niño teleconnection areas (e.g., see FEWS NET[El Niño resources](https://fews.net/el-nino-2023-2024)and CM4EW[August 2023 Special Report](https://cropmonitor.org/documents/SPECIAL/reports/Special_Report_20230824_El_Nino.pdf)).

The GEOGLAM Extended Outlook communicated expectations for subnational end-of-season maize conditions using information available in early January (Figure 9). It  was based on expert analysis of multiple factors, including CHIRPS data, a WRSI outlook, onset of seasonal rainfall, seasonal precipitation and soil moisture forecasts, occurrence of extreme heat, previous El Niño yield outcomes, and in-season crop yield forecasts from NASA Harvest and UCSB CHC.

![Figure 9. GEOGLAM Extended Outlook for main season 2023-2024 end of season maize conditions, projected based on information available before January 12th, 2024.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/GEOGLAM-Extended-Outlook-1.png)

**Mid-season drought unveiled by forecasts, satellite, and ground data**

During the season, close monitoring of rainfall performance and agro climatic impacts involves many resources, including preliminary and final CHIRPS data, daily-updated RFE2 data, MODIS and VIIRS satellite NDVI data, crop water balance and hydrologic modeling, and more. Model forecasts from a variety of sources covering weather (days to a week+), sub seasonal (weeks to a month+), and seasonal (months+) time scales are monitored to gauge what may be ahead, and if developing areas of concern may worsen or improve.

In January 2024, agro climatic monitors already had concerns about the late start to seasonal rains, while seasonal forecasts continued to indicate below-normal rainfall in Southern Africa associated with the ongoing El Niño. Then, in mid-January 2024, the GEFS, ECMWF, and[SubX models](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/shrad/SubX/Plots/Archive/20240118/S_Africa/Monthly_pr_sfc_S_Africa_IC_20240118.png)predicted a highly concerning dry spell. These forecasts collectively pointed to below-average rainfall for consecutive weeks and hotter-than-average[daytime high temperatures](https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/people/shrad/SubX/Plots/Archive/20240118/S_Africa/Monthly_tasmax_2m_S_Africa_IC_20240118.png). Agreement coming from multiple modeling systems supported a high level of concern, which was broadcast in the GEOGLAM Crop Monitor for Early Warning (Figure 10).

![Figure 10. Warnings of highly concerning hot and dry model forecasts in GEOGLAM Crop Monitor for Early Warning. Graphic from the Southern Africa outlook, published Feb 1st, 2024.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/CM4EW-Feb-2024-report-small-1.png)

The Zimbabwe Meteorological Services Department issued a dry spell alert in January that was further communicated by the Southern Africa Development Community (SADC) Agromet bulletin. As the extreme nature of the dry spell unfolded, a SADC special issue urged stakeholders to*‘urgently coordinate and implement integrated strategies for water resource management, conduct comprehensive assessments of crop and livestock conditions, ensure immediate support to affected communities, and evaluate current and forecast regional cereal staple stock levels until the 2025 harvest.’*FEWS NET organized multi-agency teams to assess the situation in February and March and learn from the in-country expertise in Zimbabwe, South Africa, and Malawi.

Satellite and gauge-based rainfall data showed that, week after week, central Southern Africa had received minimal to very below-average rainfall. CHC Early Estimates, using available final and preliminary CHIRPS data,[provided early warning](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-03-08/food-supplies-threatened-in-southern-africa-after-driest-february-on-record?utm_source=website&utm_medium=share&utm_campaign=twitter?sref=atOKgCol)that the extended dry spell was historically extreme. CHIRPS final data for February 2024 confirmed this using[rainfall gauge observations](https://wiki.chc.ucsb.edu/CHIRPS_Reality_Checks#February_2024)in Zimbabwe from the Meteorological Service Department, as well as regional gauge observations fromSouthern African Science Service Centre for Climate Change and Adaptive Land Management (SASSCAL) and other national and global observation networks. Reports that were blended into satellite-derived precipitation estimates are displayed in Figure 11.

![Figure 11. Left – CHC Early Warning Explorer showing February 2024 CHIRPS rainfall total (mm) with gauge observations overlaid as boxes. Box color shows reported rainfall and symbols indicate source. Right – February 2024 rainfall anomalies.](https://blog.chc.ucsb.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Rchecks-feb-2024-small.png)

The impacts of the 2023-24 season’s hot and dry conditions have been widespread and varied across growing environments, crop types, and access to resources. Shorter effective growing periods, yield-reducing stress, and crop losses have been reported or indicated by monitoring data in rainfed areas. There is now higher dependence on irrigated production areas and energy usage to support these. Conversely, low flows along the Zambezi River are forcing more power cuts.

The President of [Zambia declared a national disaster](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2024/2/29/zambia-declares-national-disaster-after-drought-devastates-agriculture)at the end of February, reporting the losses of approximately 1 million hectares of maize crops— about half of their planted areas. Areas reportedly saw no rain for 5 weeks. Impacts of the drought will reach more than a million farming households. Current NDVI data for some of the hardest hit areas of southern Zambia speak to these— vegetation greenness rapidly diminished following the extreme dry spell (Figure 12 top right).

On March 23rd, the President of Malawi [declared a State of Disaster](https://abcnews.go.com/International/wireStory/malawi-zambia-declaring-drought-disaster-el-nio-brings-108445104) in 23 out of 28 districts affected by El Niño conditions, due to  “late and erratic onset of rains … inadequate rains, floods, and prolonged dry spells, which have severely damaged crops and the prospects of food production.” More than 749 thousand hectares of maize were reportedly impacted— 44% of the national crop area. A preliminary assessment identified “that close to 2 million farming households have been affected.”

Due to reported and observed impacts of dryness in February, expectations for maize yields in South Africa were downgraded in the USDA FAS IPAD 2023-2024 mid-season assessment (Figure 12 bottom right). Being a maize high producer and exporter, South Africa’s production levels will be important for the flow of maize into regional markets.

Agro climatic monitoring datasets have spoken loudly about the serious nature of the Southern Africa drought (Figure 12). Most areas of the region are on track for below-normal seasonal rainfall totals and continued very low soil moisture, based on estimates and outlooks in mid-March. In the latest satellite NDVI imagery, which provides an independent perspective of observed vegetation response, reduced vegetation productivity is below-average across many regional food production areas, from southern Zambia to South Africa.
